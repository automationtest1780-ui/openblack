## Planning sheet (gacha 14.2)

**Player fantasy:** you watch the cleverest man in Saltreach put a whole town to bed, and you are one of the few still out when something walks into the harbor.

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**
- **Ivo** wants a quiet harbor after the loft stair: no crowds at the slip, no crowds at dusk, nobody talking about the tower. Obstacle: a town that is afraid of the sky and of a girl, and a marsh that lives on glass money. Cost: he takes the pans' only money and the harbor women's own words, and it works. Mags won't count him. Nobody but five people sees the first Tide-Plumbs, because he told everyone else to stay in.
- **Sera** wants every mark exact, now that the town has been told not to look. Obstacle: the wall is nearly out, and the only new thing worth writing tonight is standing in front of her in Bram's coat. Cost: she lays Wren's height down on the last course like a tide, and when Bram calls her on it she has no answer.
- **Bram** wants Wren kept close and the day kept small. Obstacle: the town looks at the tower, a councillor names her in session, and Sera reaches for the chalk. Cost: he says the thing he never says to Sera, and she doesn't answer.
- **Wren** wants to be forgiven by somebody, and counted by Sera. Obstacle: she isn't allowed down to the lofts, and she's afraid of her own light. Cost: she gets what she asked for. She is counted, and the counting goes on the wall.

## Script

### Scene 1 — The lamp room, morning
[Morning on the salt-stained glass. The chalk wall is full to the floor except for one course of stone at the bottom, by the door. Its first entry is a small "19".]
[SERA kneels at the bottom course with her tide book open, writing nothing. WREN stands at the glass in Bram's coat. It fits her now: cuffs at her wrists, no rolls.]
[The Keeper comes up the last turn of the stair with the breakwater logbook under one arm and a folded note in one hand.]
[Objective: Go down to the customs house by ten.]
WREN: Keeper! You're up! Did the lamp gutter? Was it four again?
KEEPER: Four.
WREN: Jory says four's respectable. Is respectable good? It sounds tired.
KEEPER: It's good for a lamp.
WREN: Then I'll be respectable too. When I'm tired.
[She turns back to the glass. Down below, the harbor is waking: boats, slips, smoke from Hester's vents.]
WREN: From up here the harbor's a hand. Cupped. Look, the breakwater's the thumb.
WREN: Is somebody holding it? It looks held.
[Nobody answers. She laughs, quickly, as if she'd made a joke.]
WREN: That was a joke. Was it? It came out of my mouth like one.
SERA: Keeper. What's the note?
KEEPER: Council. Ten o'clock. Item one, oil.
SERA: Oil. Not tides.
KEEPER: It says oil.
SERA: Then he's not asking about the tides. Good. He'd only write "weather" under them.
[She looks down at the slips. The water is already high against the posts where her tables are nailed.]
SERA: It came in early again. I don't know how early.
KEEPER: You always know.
SERA: I know when I've a table to know it against. I'm not guessing. I don't guess.
[Footsteps on the stair, heavy, with a clank. BRAM backs up the last steps with a loaf and a jug.]
BRAM: Bread. Milk. And the ninth step's still sulking. Morning, everybody still here.
WREN: Bram! Can I go down to the lofts today? To see Tam? I'll be very careful.
[BRAM sets the jug down. Slowly. He doesn't look at her straight away.]
BRAM: Not today, Sparrow.
WREN: You called me that yesterday. After.
BRAM: Did I.
WREN: Is it because I'm small? I'm not small any more. The coat fits.
BRAM: It's because you're mine. Sparrows are the ones that stay near the house.
WREN: Then can I go near the house? The lofts are near a house. Hester's is under them.
BRAM: Today's an up-here day. Hester's got Tam. Hester's got everybody.
WREN: Does he hate me? I'd hate me. Is that how it works?
BRAM: Tam's never hated anything in his life. He tried once with a dogfish. Gave up.
WREN: His hands went white where I held them. Then red. Then Hester came.
[She looks at her own hands, free of the sleeves. She puts them in the coat pockets.]
WREN: I told him it was hot. He held on anyway. Why does anybody hold on?
BRAM: Eat your bread. Here. Both ends, like you like.
KEEPER: He'll want to see you. When his hands are better.
WREN: Tell him I'm still sorry. With my whole mouth. He said sorry's done. I don't think it's done.
[She turns to the wall, where Sera is kneeling.]
WREN: Sera. I came up the whole stair this morning. A hundred and six. Is that close? It's closer.
SERA: It's a hundred and eight.
WREN: Then I'm nearly right! Did you count me coming up? Did you count it?
SERA: I heard you. You weren't counting out loud.
WREN: I did it inside. It's quieter inside. My legs are longer, so the steps are shorter. Is that how it works?
[SERA looks at her. At the cuffs at her wrists, the hem at her knees. The look goes on a moment too long.]
SERA: Stand still a minute.
BRAM: Sera. Bread.
[SERA looks at Bram. She goes back to the wall.]
[She takes the bread. Down on the quay, a knot of women at the slip stop work and look up at the tower. One points. Another pulls her hand down.]
WREN: Keeper. The women on the slip are looking up here.
WREN: Are they looking at the lamp room? Or at me in it?
BRAM: They're looking at the weather, love. Everybody's looking at the weather this week.
SERA: It's nine in the morning. There's no weather.
BRAM: Then they're looking at the view. It's a grand view. I've always said so.
[SERA looks at Bram, then down at the women, then at Wren at the glass. She doesn't say anything. She writes a short line in her tide book and closes it.]
SERA: Keeper. Go to his council. Come back and tell me what he called it this time.
[Objective: Go down to the slip.]
[Skip summary: The morning after Wren burned Tam's hands, the keeper brings a summons to the lamp room: council at ten, item one, oil. Wren's coat fits now. She wants to go down and see Tam, and Bram, calling her Sparrow, keeps her in. Sera can't say how early the tide is any more. The women on the slip look up at the tower.]

### Scene 2 — The slip
[The main slip below the customs house. Boats getting ready; the gutting tables out; women in a row at them with knives. Sera's tide table is nailed to the post at the top of the slip, as it is at every slip.]
[A girl of seventeen comes down the slip with a full apron, clinking.]
[Objective: Get to the customs house.]
CORRA: Luck for the boat! Penny a piece! Fell out of the sky, didn't it?
CORRA: Fresh this morning! Off my own pan! Can't get luckier!
[Nobody on the boats looks up. A deckhand turns his back on her.]
CORRA: You bought one last week! You kissed it!
DECKHAND: I threw it over the side last night. After the loft stair.
CORRA: That's a penny you threw over the side! That's half a loaf in the harbor!
DECKHAND: Then the fish can have the luck. I've had enough of it.
[CORRA rattles the apron at the next boat. Nell Garrow is on the deck of Patience, coiling a line. She slaps the rail without looking up.]
NELL: Not on my boat, Vey. Done.
CORRA: You bought luck the first week! Everybody did!
NELL: Everybody was daft the first week. I've been cured.
CORRA: It's only glass, Skipper!
NELL: So's a bottle. I don't keep a bottle in the wheelhouse either.
[At the gutting tables, a woman with a baby on her hip puts down her knife: ADA MARR. The others stop too.]
ADA: Corra Vey. Take it home.
CORRA: I'm selling, Ada. Same as you sell fish.
ADA: Fish don't burn people's hands off.
CORRA: Glass didn't burn anybody! It's cold! Feel it, it's cold!
ADA: It came out the same hole as her up the tower. Didn't it?
[A murmur along the tables. Somebody looks up at the headland. Somebody else says, "Don't."]
CORRA: That's got nothing to do with my pan.
ADA: Tam Rook's hands have. I was at that table. I watched.
ADA: She lit up like a lamp and grew. Her hands on his. When it went, she went.
ADA: She grows when the sky moves. Everybody saw.
CORRA: Then go and shout at the sky! I'm only selling glass!
[ADA reaches out and slaps the apron. It tips. Pale green glass goes across the wet stone, and some of it into the water.]
CORRA: Oi! That's a week! That's a week of bread!
ADA: Don't look up after supper. And don't buy what fell.
[The other women say it after her, under their breath, like a grace: "Don't look up after supper."]
[CORRA drops to her knees on the slip, picking glass out of the fish-wash with both hands.]
[Old Pell, mending a float on an upturned boat, puts down his knife.]
PELL: Ada. That's a girl's week you've just tipped in the harbor.
ADA: Then she can pick a better trade, Pell.
PELL: There's no better trade on the pans. That's why she's picking glass.
ADA: You'd buy it, would you? After Tam?
PELL: I'd not buy it. I'd not tip it in the harbor, neither. Nobody asked the harbor.
[On the harbor wall, two small girls are skipping a rope. They've changed the rhyme.]
[Children's voices: "Ploughman, Ploughman, where's your belt? / Don't look up, or you'll be felt."]
PELL: That's new. Hasn't even got a day on it.
PELL: Doesn't scan, either. They'll fix it by Friday. They always fix it.
[The Keeper kneels on the wet stone to help.]
CORRA: Keep. Keep, don't. It's mine. I'll do it.
CORRA: It's salt money. It's only salt money. It's only glass.
[Above them, on the customs house steps, a tall man in a magistrate's coat stands with a plain notebook open. He has been there a while. He writes one line and closes it without looking down.]
IVO: Miss Vey.
CORRA: I'm not doing anything! I'm picking up!
IVO: Then pick it up and go home, please. Keeper. Ten o'clock. You are nearly late.
[He goes in. CORRA watches the door shut. She stands with a fistful of wet glass.]
CORRA: Did he see that? He saw that. He just wrote it down.
CORRA: Write it down, then! Price of a week, in the harbor! Write that!
[She stuffs the glass into the apron and walks up the slip fast, without looking at anyone.]
[Skip summary: At the slip, the boats won't buy Corra's crater glass any more. Nell won't have it aboard. Ada Marr says it came out of the same hole as the girl in the tower, knocks Corra's apron out of her hands, and says the harbor's new saying: "Don't look up after supper." Ivo watches from the customs house steps and writes one line.]

### Scene 3 — The smokehouse, before ten
[Hester's smokehouse, mid-morning. Half the benches full. By the fire, someone asleep under a coat with both hands held up off the bench, wrapped in white cloth.]
[Objective: Hear what the harbor's saying.]
HESTER: Keeper. You look like a herring that's been told bad news. Sit.
HESTER: Quiet, mind. That's Tam by the fire. Don't wake him. He'll only tell you it'll mend.
KEEPER: How are his hands?
HESTER: In honey and linen. Up off the bench like a dog begging. Three weeks if he sits still. Six, since he won't.
HESTER: He can't hold a needle. He tried at six this morning. I took it off him.
KEEPER: Did he say anything? About Wren?
HESTER: He asked was she all right. First thing. Before his hands.
HESTER: I told him she's up the tower with Bram. He said good. Then he went back to sleep.
[She wipes her hands on her apron, twice, and sets down a plate.]
HESTER: You've come from the slip. I heard it from here. Ada Marr's got a voice like a gull with a grudge, when it's her bairns.
KEEPER: They knocked Corra's glass in the harbor.
HESTER: Course they did. Town wants something to blame it can reach. Can't reach the sky.
HESTER: Can't reach the tower either. Bram's on the stair.
KEEPER: "Don't look up after supper." Where does that come from?
HESTER: Ada's. Her house rule, since the night it went over at two. She had it in here the day before yesterday.
HESTER: Then last night half the harbor stood in my door to watch me dress his hands.
HESTER: Ada said it again in the door, and it went round the women like a cold. Out my step and down every slip.
HESTER: I said it'd be round the harbor by morning. It was. By noon the children'll be skipping it.
NET-HAULER: It's good advice. I've not looked up since. Walked into a post.
HESTER: You walked into a post before, you great cod. You've a face shaped for it.
NET-HAULER: Only it's sense, Hester. Sky moves after supper. Every time. You don't look, you don't see it go.
HESTER: And then what? It's not gone, just because you've not seen it.
NET-HAULER: It's gone for me.
HESTER: That's the trouble with this town. It thinks shutting its eyes is a kind of roof.
[A soft-coated man at the end of the near bench sets down a cup he hasn't touched. One hand goes to the small brass bell on his watch-chain, and stays there without ringing it.]
TEODOR: Mrs Pike. If you'll allow me, your saying.
HESTER: It's not mine. It's a kitchen saying. Nobody owns a kitchen saying.
TEODOR: Of course. I only meant to remark on it.
TEODOR: In Bellwater we have a saying too. Don't look up after supper. How curious that you have it.
[HESTER stops wiping. She looks at him a long moment.]
HESTER: Do you, now.
TEODOR: Oh, a very old one. My grandmother said it. Bellwater rains a great deal, you understand.
HESTER: What's rain got to do with looking up?
TEODOR: Very little, Mrs Pike. That's rather what I always thought.
HESTER: Mr Lisle. I've known you a fortnight, and that's the first thing you've said that told me anything.
TEODOR: Ah. Then I'd better stop. I'm told I'm bad at it.

TEODOR: The council sits at ten. I thought I might listen. I'm told it's the custom to stand at the back.

### Scene 4 — The customs house, the session
[The customs house. Seven chairs under the window. The benches full: net-haulers, gutting women, Pell on the second bench, BRAM on the end of the front bench with his unlit storm lantern between his boots. TEODOR stands at the back by the door, hat in his hands.]
[The keeper's chair, the one with the bad leg, is empty by the door.]
[Objective: Hear the two items.]
[A bell strikes ten. On the stroke, IVO comes in with the green minute book under his arm and sits in the middle chair.]
IVO: The harbor council is in session. Two items. Oil, then glass.
IVO: The chair will be brief. Everyone here has a supper to get home to.
HARBORMASTER: Before your two, Magistrate. There's a third. Everyone in this room is thinking it.
IVO: The chair has two items, Harbormaster.
GAGE: The girl, Magistrate.
[The room goes quiet. Every head on the benches turns a little toward the end of the front bench. BRAM looks at his lantern.]
GAGE: The one up the old tower. Tam Rook's hands.
GAGE: My wife was on the loft stair. She says the girl lit up and grew. While she watched.
GAGE: She says she grows when the sky moves. Half the harbor's saying it now.
HARBORMASTER: My lad won't go up the headland path. Not for money. Not for a dare.
CHANDLER: I've had three mothers in my shop asking if it's catching.
IVO: Is it, Chandler?
CHANDLER: How would I know? I sell rope.
IVO: Then you told them no. Good. That is the correct answer in a rope shop.
GAGE: That's not an answer, Magistrate. That's a shop.
[IVO lays his pen down flat beside the book.]
IVO: Councillor. The council does not minute children. It never has.
IVO: Tam Rook's hands are burned. Mrs Pike has dressed them. Tam Rook has not complained to this council.
BRAM: He wouldn't. He'd say it'll mend.
IVO: The benches do not speak in session, Hollis.
BRAM: I'm not speaking. I'm agreeing with you. Loudly.
IVO: Then the chair will minute it as muttering.
IVO: The council will deal with what it can count. Item one. Oil.
IVO: The harbor fund holds oil for eleven weeks. The gales have not begun.
IVO: From today the chandler sells household oil at one gill a house a night. No more.
CHANDLER: One gill! Folk burn three on a dark night, Magistrate. More, with little ones.
IVO: One gill burns an hour. That is supper, and an hour after. That is enough for any house in Saltreach.
IVO: The harbor fund buys the rest of your stock, Chandler, at cost. For the breakwater.
CHANDLER: At cost.
IVO: At cost. In February, cost will look like a great deal of money.
CHANDLER: ...It will. I've the figures. It will.
IVO: The quay lanterns and the slip lamps will not be lit after supper.
HARBORMASTER: And the boats coming in late? In the dark, with no quay lamps?
IVO: They come in on the breakwater light, as they always have. Keeper. Your lamp is lit dusk to dawn?
[Choice a: "Every night."]
[Choice b: "Unless the oil runs out."]
[Choices converge.]
IVO: Then the boats have a light. The quay does not need one. Nobody needs to be on the quay after supper.
PELL: Folk'll be in their beds by nine.
IVO: Yes, Mr Pell. That is rather the idea.
PELL: You heard that one.
IVO: The chair hears what is useful. Item two. Crater glass.
IVO: This morning there was a crowd on the slip over a girl's apron. The keeper saw it. So did I.
IVO: A crowd on the slip talks. Then it talks about the sky. Then it talks about the tower.
IVO: For public order, the sale of crater glass stops. At the slip, at market, by private hand. Until the council says otherwise.
HARBORMASTER: That's the pans' money, Magistrate. Mags Teller's got twenty on those pans.
IVO: The chair is aware what it costs, Harbormaster. It has done the sum.
GAGE: Abel Thorn'll be pleased. He's wanted it in the hole since the first morning.
IVO: The council is not burying anything, Councillor. Burying costs money. Stopping costs nothing.
[At the back, TEODOR lifts a hand, politely, the way a man asks for the salt.]
TEODOR: If you'll allow me, Magistrate. A question from a guest.
IVO: Mr Lisle.
TEODOR: The glass I have already bought. I should like to know where the council would prefer it to be.
IVO: Where it is, Mr Lisle. Glass already bought may stay with its owner.
IVO: It may not be sold on. It may not leave Saltreach.
TEODOR: Ah. Then it shall stay with me. I am in no hurry at all.
IVO: You are seldom in a hurry, Mr Lisle. I have noted it. Nine days of it.
TEODOR: News travels at its own pace, Magistrate. So, I find, do I.
[TEODOR sits down on the end of the back bench, entirely at ease. Ivo watches him a beat longer than the room does.]
IVO: One thing more. The council would like to advise residents. Something short. For the door.
IVO: "Weather" has done well. It would like company.
[A pause. ADA MARR stands up at the back, the baby asleep on her shoulder.]
ADA: We've got one already.
IVO: Go on.
ADA: Don't look up after supper. That's my house. And every slip's, since the loft stair.
ADA: It's not the council's. It's ours. I'm only saying it so you know.
[IVO picks up his pen.]
IVO: Thank you. That is the word.
[BRAM looks up from his lantern, at Ivo. Ivo doesn't look back.]
IVO: Those in favor. Item one, the oil. Item two, the glass. And the advice to residents, as given from the benches.
[Hands rise along the table. The harbormaster's comes up last. Gage's comes up slowly, and stays up.]
IVO: Six. The chair does not vote. Carried.
[He writes. Then he reads it aloud, in the same voice he used for the price of salt.]
IVO: "Household oil, one gill a house a night. Quay and slip lamps dark after supper. No sale of crater glass, for public order."
IVO: "Residents are advised: don't look up after supper."
[On the benches, nobody says anything. Then a woman near the front nods. Then another. It goes along the rows like a tide.]
[Skip summary: In council, Gage raises Wren and Tam's hands; Ivo says the council doesn't minute children and moves to his two items. He rations household lamp oil to one gill a house a night and darkens the quay after supper, and he stops all sale of crater glass "for public order". Teodor is told his glass may stay but not leave. ### Scene 5 — The customs house door
[The harbor road outside. IVO comes out with a hammer, a nail and two copied sheets. He pins the first to the board, under the black-ink notice: "Weather. The council is watching it."]
[Objective: Take the notice to the pans.]
IVO: Keeper. You were on time again. The chair noted it.
KEEPER: You took their saying.
IVO: I took the best one in the room. They gave it to me. You heard.
IVO: People will do what they already say. It saves a great deal of persuading.
[BRAM comes out behind them with the lantern swinging from one finger. He stops at the board and reads it with his lips moving.]
BRAM: "Don't look up after supper." In black.
IVO: In black. Red is for things we are sure of.
BRAM: You've told a whole town that steers by stars not to look up, Ivo.
IVO: After supper. Before supper they may look all they like. The council has no objection to daylight.
BRAM: You're doing that face where you've already won.
IVO: It is my only face, Hollis.
BRAM: It's not. I've seen the other one. You had it on in there, when Gage said her name.
[IVO taps the nail flat. Twice.]
IVO: Nobody minuted her name. That was the point of the oil.
BRAM: ...Was it.
IVO: Ask your lodger whether she would rather be an item, or have the town in bed by nine.
IVO: I find the second is cheaper for everybody.
BRAM: She's not my lodger. She's Wren.
IVO: Then keep Wren up the tower, Hollis. For a week. Two. Until they are bored of her.
BRAM: Saltreach doesn't get bored. It gets quiet. Not the same thing.
BRAM: One gill a house. What's my lantern, then? Is that a house?
IVO: Your lantern is a lantern.
BRAM: It's where I live, mostly. I've decided it's a house. Tell the chandler.
IVO: I will tell the chandler nothing. I have not heard you.
BRAM: Good. I'll put that on your slate.
[Across the road, the smokehouse door bangs open. HESTER comes over with her hands still floury, wiping them on her apron, twice. She reads the board.]
HESTER: Ada came in and said you'd done it. I said, he never. Not a kitchen saying.
IVO: Mrs Pike.
HESTER: That's ours, Ivo Thane. It's a kitchen saying. It came off my step with Tam's burns still on my cloths.
HESTER: And you've gone and took it, and put a nail through it.
IVO: It was offered from the benches, Mrs Pike. By Mrs Marr. In session.
HESTER: Ada would offer you her teeth if you asked in that voice.
IVO: I did not ask for her teeth. Only her advice. It was good advice.
HESTER: It was good advice on a step. On a door, it's a rule.
HESTER: Council decides what goes in the minutes. This room decides what goes in the town. You told me that once, near enough.
IVO: You told me that, Mrs Pike. At the herring, on a Thursday.
HESTER: Well. Now you've minuted the town. How's that sit?
IVO: Quietly, I hope. By nine.
[HESTER looks at him a long moment. Then she takes out her small greasy book and writes in it.]
IVO: What are you writing?
HESTER: You. On the slate. Under "took".
HESTER: And Thursday's herring's going up a penny. On account of oil.
[She goes back across the road and bangs the door. BRAM laughs, once.]
[BRAM goes, toward the headland path, not hurrying. IVO watches him go. Then he holds out the second sheet to the Keeper.]
IVO: The pans must have this today. Mrs Teller will want it in writing, so she can hate it accurately.
KEEPER: Why me?
IVO: Because you are the only council coat the marsh will open a door to. You earned it on their road.
IVO: It is an unfair use of a good thing. I am aware.
[The Keeper takes the notice.]
IVO: I will be along. Later. I walk in the afternoons. You will recall.
IVO: Take a nail, Keeper. Mrs Teller's board has a nail missing. It has had one missing for six years.
[Objective: Take the notice to the salt pans.]
[Skip summary: Ivo pins the new notice under "Weather." Bram tells him he's told a town that steers by the stars not to look up. Ivo says he took the town's own words, and the oil kept Wren's name out of the minutes. He sends the keeper to the pans with a copy, because the marsh trusts them.]

### Scene 6 — The pan-house
[The salt pans at noon. White beds, the low pan-house, the board by its door with a nail missing. MAGS sits on the step with the pay-tin on her knees. Cutters and pickers stand about in the lane, eating bread. ABEL stands with the pony at the cart.]
[Objective: Hear Mags out.]
MAGS: Keeper. You've got a paper in your hand, and a face that says I won't like it.
MAGS: Give it here. I'd rather read bad news than be told it nice.
[She reads it. Her lips move on one line. She reads it again.]
MAGS: "No sale of crater glass. For public order."
MAGS: Public order. On my pans. Where's the public? Where's the order?
KEEPER: The slip, this morning. They knocked Corra's apron over.
MAGS: I know. She came in white. She's out the back, not talking. That's how I knew it was bad.
[She pulls the pencil from behind her ear and taps it on the pay-tin. Twice. Then she stands up.]
MAGS: Right. I'll tell them. Not you. Not him. Me.
MAGS: My lot's mine to tell. I've done it before. One kitchen at a time.
[She turns to the lane. The cutters stop eating.]
MAGS: Listen. Council's stopped the glass. All of it. No selling it, no buying it. Not at the slip, not anywhere.
MAGS: That means the half-share's done. And the pickers' own half's done.
[A low noise in the lane. Somebody throws down a crust.]
MAGS: Names. Abel. No glass money.
ABEL: Never wanted any.
MAGS: I know, Abel. You're still on the list. The Dunn boys. No glass money.
DUNN BOY: What about the shed roof? You said glass money'd do the shed roof.
MAGS: Shed roof's a shed roof. It's leaked forty years. It'll leak forty-one.
MAGS: Ferris. No glass money. Your coal's paid to Christmas. After that, we'll see.
FERRIS: I'll wear my hat in bed. I've done it before.
MAGS: You've worn it in bed since your wedding, Ferris. Corra.
[Silence. MAGS looks at the pan-house door.]
MAGS: Corra Vey. No glass money.
[The back door bangs. Nobody comes through it.]
MAGS: She heard. Me. No glass money.
MAGS: That's my lot. Fair's fair.
MAGS: It's rubbish. But it's fair. It's the same rubbish for everybody.
CUTTER: Fair! He's took the only money this marsh has seen in two years!
MAGS: He has. And I sold the half-share to a man who never haggles, and I said yes to the coal. I'm in this too.
CUTTER: You didn't stop it.
MAGS: No. I counted it. That's my job. Somebody else's job was to stop it, and he's done it.
MAGS: Back to the pans. Salt's still salt. Salt's still money, just smaller money.
[The cutters go, slowly, without talking. MAGS sits back down on the step. She doesn't open the tin.]
KEEPER: Did you want to be the one to tell them?
MAGS: I didn't want anybody to have to. But it was going to be someone, so it's me.
MAGS: My feet have opinions about it. I've told them to wait their turn.
[ABEL comes over from the cart, the pony behind him without a lead.]
ABEL: Mags.
MAGS: Don't say you said so, Abel. I've had a morning.
ABEL: I wasn't going to say it. I was going to say it's not burying.
ABEL: Stopping's not burying. It's still out there. It's still in the buyer's back room. It's still in Corra's apron.
MAGS: I know.
ABEL: Reeds leaned again. This morning. Toward the town, this time.
MAGS: I know, Abel. Cut them anyway.
[ABEL looks at the keeper, at the council coat, then at the empty nail on the board.]
ABEL: You'll be wanting to pin that.
KEEPER: He gave me a nail.
ABEL: Course he did. He thinks of everything, that one. Except what it costs at this end.
[The Keeper holds out the notice and the nail.]
MAGS: Leave it. I'll pin it. It's my board. If anybody spits on it, I want it to be me.
[Skip summary: At the pan-house, Mags reads the council's notice and tells her cutters herself, name by name, that there's no glass money: the shed roof, Ferris's coal after Christmas, Corra's share. A cutter blames the magistrate; Mags says she's in it too. Abel says stopping isn't burying, and the reeds leaned toward the town this morning.]

### Scene 7 — The pan-house door
[Round the back of the pan-house, out of the wind. The back door is shut. CORRA sits against it with her knees up and the apron beside her, damp, clinking. She has a stub of chalk in her hand.]
[Objective: Find Corra.]
CORRA: Go away, Keep. No. Don't. Sit down. Not there, that's wet.
CORRA: Did Mags read it out? Did she say my name?
KEEPER: She said everyone's.
CORRA: Course she did. She'd read her own name out at her own funeral. "Mags. Dead. Fair's fair."
CORRA: That's Mum's rent. That's Friday. That's two shillings and I've got the bit I picked off the slip.
CORRA: Wet glass. That's what I've got. Can't sell it. Can't eat it. Can't even sell it to a deckhand to throw in the harbor.
KEEPER: What will your mum do?
CORRA: Say we'll manage. She says it every time. We never do.
CORRA: Then she'll go to Hester's and not ask, and Hester'll put us on the slate and not say.
CORRA: I hate the slate. I was nearly off it. I was nearly off it, Keep.
[She rattles the apron. Then she takes out one piece, looks at it, and puts it back.]
CORRA: It's not warm. Mine never are. Not like hers. The girl's.
CORRA: Ada said it came out the same hole. As her.
[She doesn't look at the keeper. She looks at the chalk.]
CORRA: Is she all right? The girl. Wren. I'm not asking for me. Mags'll ask, and I'll want to know.
KEEPER: She wanted to go down to Tam. Bram won't let her.
CORRA: No. He wouldn't.
CORRA: Everybody's scared of her now. Ada's scared. The boats are scared. I'm not scared.
CORRA: I'm not. I'm only... I'd not hold her hand. That's all. That's different.
[She rattles the apron, hard, once.]
CORRA: Keep. What's the Belt at? Now. After the loft stair.
KEEPER: Twenty-four.
CORRA: Twenty-four. Before that was twenty-eight. Before that thirty-two. Sera said thirty-two on the quay. Before that thirty-seven.
CORRA: And forty-one's the old one. Where it sat. Forever. That's right? I've got them right?
KEEPER: You've got them right.
[CORRA gets up on her knees and writes on the pan-house door, in big round numbers: 41. 37. 32. 28. 24.]
CORRA: There.
CORRA: They can stop the glass. They can't stop me looking. I'll look after supper and all.
CORRA: I'll look every night. On my own door. Out loud.
KEEPER: Sera would say write where, not what.
CORRA: Then I'll learn where! I'm learning! That's the numbers. That's where.
[ABEL comes round the corner of the pan-house with the reed-hook on his shoulder. He stops at the door and reads it.]
ABEL: Child. What's that.
CORRA: Numbers. Sera's. Don't you dare rub them out.
ABEL: I've no wish to rub them out.
[He looks at the numbers for a long while, without looking up at the sky.]
CORRA: Abel. You think the glass does that? To people? Like Tam?
ABEL: I think it came out of the same hole.
CORRA: That's not an answer!
ABEL: It's the one I've got. Marsh gives, marsh keeps. Meaning, I don't know, and I'm frightened.
[CORRA stares at him. She's never heard him say the second half.]
CORRA: ...You've never said that bit before.
ABEL: Never had to. You've never asked me straight.
[He turns to go. Then he stops, takes off his coat, and hangs it on the door handle, under the numbers.]
ABEL: Cold behind here. Don't argue.
CORRA: I've still got your other one!
ABEL: Then you've two. Fine. Fine, you'd say.
[He goes. CORRA looks at the coat on the handle. She doesn't put it on. She doesn't take it down either.]
[Skip summary: Behind the pan-house, Corra counts what the glass stop costs her family: her mum's rent, due Friday. She asks the keeper for the Belt's bearings and chalks them on the pan-house door, 41 to 24, so she can look every night. Abel asks if the glass does that to people, and admits he doesn't know and is frightened.]

### Scene 8 — The north road
[The north road in the early afternoon, the reeds leaning toward the town. A tall figure in a magistrate's coat walks out along it from the harbor, alone, gloved.]
[On the pan-house step, MAGS sees him coming. She stands up. In the lane the cutters see him too, and one by one they turn their backs and go on cutting.]
[Objective: Walk back to town.]
IVO: Mrs Teller.
MAGS: Magistrate. You've come to see it pinned.
IVO: I walk in the afternoons. I came to see the pans.

IVO: Thank you.

MAGS: My people are cutting. Cutting faces that way today.
IVO: I see.

IVO: No.

ABEL: I wanted it buried. You've stopped it selling. That's not the same.

ABEL: The council has oil. Eleven weeks of it, I hear. And it's keeping it.
IVO: For the breakwater. Boats come in on it.
ABEL: Boats came in on the old tower, before you shut it. Nobody's ever buried a thing in this town that cost money.

ABEL: Corra's chalked the sky on the back door. Go and look, if you walk in the afternoons. You'll not like it.
[ABEL goes on toward the reed beds. Coming the other way along the road, a soft-coated man walks out from the town with his hat in his hand, unhurried, as if on a promenade.]
TEODOR: Magistrate. Keeper. A fine afternoon for the marsh.
IVO: Mr Lisle. You have nothing to buy here. The council saw to it this morning.
TEODOR: Quite so. I came to look at it. Looking is still permitted before supper, I understand.
IVO: Before supper it is encouraged.
TEODOR: Then I am entirely within the law. How restful.
[He looks along the pans, at the turned backs, at Mags on the step with her tin.]
TEODOR: Mrs Teller looks as if she would like to throw that tin at someone.
IVO: She would like to throw it at me. She is too fair to do it.
TEODOR: Ah. Fairness. A great inconvenience in a business partner. I admire it enormously.
IVO: You have lost your trade, Mr Lisle. You do not seem troubled.
TEODOR: I have lost a little time, Magistrate. I have a great deal of it.
TEODOR: The glass will keep. It is very good at keeping. That is rather the nature of glass.
IVO: And you will keep with it.
TEODOR: Where else would I go? You have told me it may not leave. I am terribly obedient.
[His hand goes to the small brass bell on his watch-chain. He doesn't ring it.]
TEODOR: Good afternoon, Magistrate. I hope the oil lasts the winter. Truly.
[He goes on toward the pans. Mags, on the step, watches him come, and does not stand up.]
[IVO stands a moment. Then he turns to the Keeper.]
IVO: Walk back with me, Keeper. The road is long enough for two.
[They walk. The pans fall behind. Nobody on them looks up.]
IVO: Mrs Teller did not count me. I find I noticed.
KEEPER: Did you think she would?
IVO: No. I did the sum. It was in the sum.
IVO: It is different on the road than in the book. It always is. That is why I walk.
[Choice a: "Was it worth it?"]
[Choice b: "You could take it back."]
[Choices converge.]
IVO: Tonight the quay will be dark by nine, and nobody will stand under the tower and talk.
IVO: Nobody will say her name in a crowd. Nobody will throw anything at the slip.
IVO: In a week they will be bored of her. In two, of the sky. That is what the oil buys.
KEEPER: And the marsh?
IVO: The marsh will hate me for a winter. A marsh that hates me is cheaper than a harbor that runs.
IVO: I have seen a harbor run, Keeper. I have not yet seen a marsh do anything quickly.
[He walks a few paces in silence.]
IVO: Corra Vey's mother. Rent on Friday.
KEEPER: Two shillings.
IVO: Two shillings.
[He says it the way he says a vote. He doesn't write it down. He doesn't take out the notebook at all.]

### Scene 9 — The headland path, dusk
[The headland path at dusk. Below, the harbor. One by one the house lamps come on for supper; one by one, the shutters close over them. The quay lanterns stand unlit on their posts.]
[The breakwater lamp is lit and burning steady. The Keeper climbs toward the tower.]
[Objective: Climb to the lamp room.]
[BRAM comes up the path from the marsh side with the storm lantern lit, and WREN at his elbow, in the coat, close.]
BRAM: Keeper! Patrol's walked. Crater road's empty. Mags didn't come out to count me.
BRAM: First time in a week. I felt very uncounted. Like a sock.
WREN: We walked it twice! Bram said once was for you!
KEEPER: Thank you.
WREN: It was very long without you. Bram talked about soup the whole way.
BRAM: I talked about soup for half of it. The other half was pie.
KEEPER: Anything on the road?
BRAM: Nothing. Reeds leaning, like Abel said. Toward town now. Funny thing about reeds.
WREN: Corra's door has numbers on! Big ones! Abel's coat's hanging off the handle like a man!
WREN: Corra was sat under them. She waved at Bram. She didn't wave at me.
BRAM: She was busy, love.
WREN: She was looking at my hands. Everybody looks at my hands now. Are they different?
BRAM: They're the same two hands. Bigger. That's all.
[WREN stops at the bend in the path and looks down at the harbor. The shutters are closing fast now.]
WREN: Bram. Why's everybody shutting their eyes? The houses. Look, they're all doing it.
BRAM: It's supper, love. Folk shut up for supper.

BRAM: It's still up there, Sparrow.

KEEPER: It's in now.

### Scene 10 — The lamp room, dusk

BRAM: Sera. That's enough.
SERA: It's three questions.
BRAM: It's three questions about the worst night of her life. Ask the sky. It doesn't mind.

WREN: Is there a next time? There is, isn't there?

KEEPER: It came in at dusk tonight. With a haze on it.
SERA: At dusk. The table says nine. That's hours.
SERA: Write it at dusk, and at three. Both. I'll want every one.
[BRAM picks up the lantern. He trims the wick, though it doesn't need it.]
BRAM: So. Funny thing about supper. Hester's sent stew, and if it gets cold she'll know, because she always knows.
SERA: I didn't say anything about supper.
BRAM: No. Somebody had to.

WREN: Sera? There's people in the harbor.
SERA: There's nobody out. You said so on the path.
WREN: Not out. In. In the water. Standing.
WREN: There's people standing in the harbor. Is that allowed, after supper?

SERA: Plumbs.
SERA: In the water. One. Four. Seven. Nine. Eleven.
SERA: Eleven. Keeper. Mark it. Eleven, in the harbor, at the tide.
BRAM: In the harbor. In the water, this time. Last night it was the lanes.
SERA: They're not moving. They're standing. Same as the lanes.
WREN: Bram, nobody's looking at them. All the shutters are shut. Nobody knows they're there.

SERA: There's a boat.
BRAM: Who's out after supper? Who'd be out?
SERA: Small. One oar's off true. That's Pell's skiff. He pulls left.
BRAM: He'll row straight through them. He'll not see them in the dark till he's on them.
[BRAM is already at the stair with the lantern.]
BRAM: Wren. Here. With me. Don't you light.
WREN: I won't. I won't, Bram. I promise very hard.
SERA: Keeper. Bring the chalk.

### Scene 11 — The slip, the evening tide

### Scene 12 — The slip, after
[The top of the slip. Pell sits on the stone in a puddle with his cap on, coughing. Two figures lie broken in the shallows. The rest stand at the water's edge, dripping, heads turned up the quay.]
[Objective: Get Pell off the slip.]
PELL: Forty years. Forty years I've come up that slip, and nobody's ever stood in it.
BRAM: You're very wet for a man who was right.
PELL: I'm always wet. It's a boat. Get your hand off my collar, Hollis, it's my good collar.
BRAM: You've got a good collar?
PELL: I've got one collar. That makes it the good one.
KEEPER: Why were you out after supper?
PELL: My pots. I set them by the church roof and the chimney now. Can't see either after dark, can I, if the quay's dark.

### Scene 15 — The lamp room, night
[The lamp room, late. Bram's lantern on the cradle step, Hester's pot beside it with BRAM chalked on the lid. SERA kneels at the last course by the door with a coil of knotted cord in her lap.]
[The Keeper comes up the last turn of the stair. BRAM is on the top step. WREN sits under the window, drying her face on the coat cuff.]
[Objective: Eat supper. Then hold the chalk.]
BRAM: Stew. Hester's, from dusk. It went cold while we were down the slip, so I sat it on the lantern.
BRAM: She opened the door for Pell and shouted through the crack. Ration's ruined her evening. In bed by nine, like a nun.
BRAM: Eat. Everybody. It's after supper, so it's very late supper. That's still supper.
[He hands out bowls. SERA takes hers, and eats some of it hot. Once, she opens her tide book to the back page, writes something very short, and closes it.]
KEEPER: Is the stew all right?
SERA: It's hot.
BRAM: She says that like it's a reading. "Hot." Mark it.
SERA: Hot isn't a number. Don't mark it.
[BRAM laughs, once. Then he sees the cord in her lap, and stops.]
BRAM: What's that for?
SERA: My mother's cord. A knot every hand. For surveying a pan, or a slip.
SERA: Wren. Come and stand by the wall.

BRAM: Sera.
SERA: I said I'd do it. You said tomorrow. That was a week of tomorrows.
BRAM: Not tonight.

WREN: Plumbs are straight. They're the straightest thing I've ever seen.

WREN: I'm holding. I'm holding very hard. Are you counting me? Is this counting?
SERA: Fifteen hands. And a finger.
