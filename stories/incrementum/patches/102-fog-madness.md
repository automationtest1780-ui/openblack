## Planning sheet (gacha 14.2)

**Player fantasy:** a whole room votes the sky away, and you are the one who carries a second count up the stair anyway.

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**
- **Sera** wants the sightings minuted as seen, by bearing and hour. Obstacle: the chair, the oil money, and a room that would rather be dull. Cost: she's refused again in front of the town, and she bolts the one person who stood with her out of the lamp room.
- **Ivo** wants Saltreach to stay dull enough that buyers keep coming. Obstacle: five reports, a witness who doesn't drink, and a surveyor with a map. Cost: he writes "fog-madness" into the town's book with his own pen, in front of the keeper who stood at the crater with him.

- **Jory** wants to be left to watch the light. Obstacle: the town still tells his father's story in front of him. Cost: he gives the keeper the old keeper's notch and asks to be kept off the wall.

**New locked facts (for the ledger)**
- The council minuted item four, "Reports of tall figures in the north marsh," as "consistent with fog-madness": residents to rest, eat hot food and keep off the pans after dark; no further action. Six hands; the chair does not vote. Ivo wrote and read the minute himself. (WB, roadmap)
- "Fog-madness" is an old marsh word (the cooper's grandfather's) for cutters who came in off the marsh seeing tall men in the reeds. Ivo took it up in session: "That is the word."
- Sera asked three things: minute the sightings as seen, a lamp and watch on the marsh road at dusk, nobody on the marsh after dark. Refused, except the third, which Ivo kept for a different reason.
- Sera brought five reports with a bearing on each; the lines cross in the north reeds inside a quarter mile; two were at noon. Tam testified to one at noon from the loft roof.
- Sera spoke to the seats through the harbormaster and never to the chair. The minutes record that she "will come back."
- The keeper's logbook (the council's book) holds a line for a figure standing in the north marsh at a quarter past two, written at Sera's word.
- Sera tested Wren in the lamp room: names, the Belt, the shard in a cup of cold seawater (warm by a count of sixty), and "where did you come from" (Wren pointed up; Sera wrote a number she wouldn't say).
- Sera bolted the keeper out after "Why does it matter what they write down?"
- Jory and the keeper have met. Jory won't say why he came back. He watches the breakwater lamp from the bollard at the far end, dawn and dusk.
- The breakwater parapet has a sighting notch cut by the old keeper; at dusk the old tower sits in the V and the Belt used to sit on top of it "like a cap on a post." It is now one finger east. Jory showed the keeper and asked not to be named.
- Sera gave the keeper a stub of chalk of their own. The keeper's dusk count from the breakwater is now on the wall beside hers ("Hold the chalk").
- A lamp was seen at the crater after dark, where nobody goes. The keeper marked it.

## Script

### Scene 1 — The breakwater, dawn
[First grey light over the harbor. The breakwater lamp is still burning. The Keeper turns the wick down and writes in the logbook: "Lamp out at dawn. Wind light, from the north."]
[A folded paper is tucked under the logbook strap, in a small, upright hand: "Keeper to attend council. Customs house, ten o'clock. Item four. I. Thane."]
[Objective: End the night shift. Attend the council at ten.]
[At the far end of the breakwater a young man sits on a bollard with his collar up. He looks at the lamp before he looks at the Keeper.]
JORY: Out on time. Lit on time last night, too. Not that I looked.
KEEPER: You've been here all night?
JORY: Since the tide turned. Couldn't sleep. It's a free wall.
KEEPER: Who are you?
JORY: Does it matter?
[He looks at the lamp again, as if checking it has really gone out.]
JORY: Jory. Flint. You'll have heard the name, if you've eaten at Hester's.
KEEPER: The last keeper's name.
JORY: The old keeper's. Yes.
JORY: You've got his stool. His hook for the oilskin. That's his nail the book hangs on.
JORY: Don't move them on my account. They were the council's. Everything out here's the council's.
KEEPER: Why did you come back?
JORY: Packet comes in twice a week. I came in on one of them.
KEEPER: That's not what I asked.
JORY: No. It's what I'm answering.
[He nods at the lamp-house.]
JORY: Your wick's cut on a slant. It burns blue down one side.
JORY: Square it. Scissors flat, not the point. Not my business.
KEEPER: You know the lamp.
JORY: I grew up under it. You learn what a flame sounds like when it's wrong.
JORY: Did they tell you the oil's counted? Every gill. The chandler checks it against your hours.
KEEPER: Who checks the chandler?
JORY: Thane. Thane checks everybody. He'd check the tide if it signed for anything.
KEEPER: You know a lot about a job that isn't yours.
JORY: I know a lot about a stool. I sat on the floor next to it till I was nine.
JORY: Wind, boats in, boats out, lamp lit, lamp out. That's the book. Nobody reads it.
KEEPER: Pell said that. "Write it anyway."
JORY: Pell would. Pell's the only man in this town who reads anything twice.
[He looks out past the harbor mouth, where the packet boat comes in.]
JORY: I came in on the packet the night the sky dropped. Stood on the deck and watched it go over.
JORY: Dropped right over the one town I'd sworn I'd never see again. Felt about right.
KEEPER: What did it look like?
JORY: Like a lamp somebody threw. Ask the surveyor. She'll give you a number. I've only got a feeling, and that's free.
JORY: Your lamp was lit. Nobody on the breakwater. I looked.
KEEPER: I was at the marsh.
JORY: Council says there was haze that night. Funny. It was clear as glass from the packet.
JORY: Not my business. Lit, and nobody on it. Nobody'll say you slept.
[He says it lightly. He doesn't look at the Keeper while he says it.]
KEEPER: Why would they?
JORY: They wouldn't. You're new.
[He reaches over and taps the folded paper on the logbook.]
JORY: That's Thane's hand. Item four. That'll be the marsh.
JORY: Half the harbor's seen something stood in the reeds this week. Nobody's saying what.
KEEPER: Have you?
JORY: I don't go north. I don't go anywhere much. I sit here.
KEEPER: Where do you sleep?
JORY: Tam Rook's got a floor. He won't take rent. He'd mend me if I sat still long enough.
JORY: Hester's fed me every night since I got in. Hasn't written it down once. That's worse.
KEEPER: Worse?
JORY: You'd know, if you'd grown up here. Not my business to explain it.
JORY: The keeper's chair's at the front, by the door. They put you where they can see you leave.
KEEPER: You've sat in it?
JORY: Sat under it. Every session, till I was too big to fit.
[He stands, turns his collar up, and glances once more at the dark lamp.]
JORY: Ten o'clock. Thane starts on the stroke and minutes who's late.
JORY: Square the wick before you go. It'll want lighting again at dusk, and I'll be here. Not looking.
[He walks off the breakwater toward the town, without looking at it.]
[Objective: Go to the customs house.]
[Skip summary: At dawn, ending a night shift, the keeper finds a summons to the council's session on the marsh sightings. Jory Flint, son of the last breakwater keeper and back in town since the night of the fall, is watching the lamp. He knows the job and won't say why he came back.]

### Scene 2 — The customs house, before the session
[The customs house: one long whitewashed room with a tide table nailed by the door. Seven chairs stand at a table under the window. The town's benches are already half full.]
[Objective: Take the keeper's chair. Wait for item four.]
[Tam Rook sits on the front bench in a clean jersey, a netting needle between his teeth. He takes it out to speak.]
TAM: Oh, Keeper. Hang on. There. Hello.
TAM: You're by the door. That one. The chair with the bad leg. It's the keeper's.
KEEPER: Are you here for item four?
TAM: I am item four. Some of it. I saw one, from the loft roof. Harbormaster wants me to say so to the seats.
TAM: I've never said anything to the seats. I said something to the harbormaster once, about his net.
KEEPER: What happened?
TAM: He didn't like it. It was true, though. It had a hole you could drive a cow through.
KEEPER: Who are the seats?
TAM: Seven of them. Harbormaster, he's boats. Chandler, she's rope and oil and what everything costs.
TAM: Cooper, he's barrels. He's old. He was old when I was little, and he's kept at it.
TAM: Three more who never talk. And the magistrate in the middle chair, writing it all down.
TAM: Sera's up front. She's been here since eight. She won't sit.
[At the front, Sera Vant stands by the wall with a long roll of paper under one arm. She is chalking small marks on the back of her hand.]
SERA: Keeper. Good. They'll ask you. Say what you saw.
SERA: Bearing and hour. Not what it looked like. Not how it felt. Where, and when.
KEEPER: What if they ask how it felt?
SERA: Then say it was a quarter past whatever, bearing whatever, and sit down.
SERA: Feelings are what they want. They can call a feeling madness. They can't call a bearing anything but a bearing.
KEEPER: What's on the paper?
SERA: The north marsh. Five reports this week. I walked to every one and stood where the witness stood.
SERA: Pan-house roof. The north channel. The reed beds, twice. The loft roof at noon. That's Tam's.
TAM: That's mine! She came up the loft ladder to ask where I'd been sitting. Nobody comes up the ladder.
SERA: You were four feet left of the chimney. It matters.
TAM: I know! I just didn't think anybody else knew.
[Old Pell sits on the second bench with his cap on his knee.]
PELL: Morning, Keeper. Come to watch her ask twice?
KEEPER: Twice?
PELL: She stood up there when the Belt slipped. Brought her numbers. They wrote "haze."
PELL: Now she's back with more numbers. Brave or daft. At my age it's the same thing.
SERA: Pell. If they ask, you'll say your pots.
PELL: They won't ask. But I'll say them anyway, under my breath, so I've said them.
TAM: Is it true they've a word ready? For what we saw?
PELL: Council's always got a word ready. That's what a council's for.
TAM: What do I do with my hands? When I'm stood up?
SERA: Point. At where it was.
TAM: There's no marsh in here to point at.
SERA: Then point north. North is north in a customs house.
TAM: Is it? Course it is. Sorry. I'm all knots this morning.
[Outside the open window, two children on the harbor wall are chanting as they skip.]
[Children's voices: "Ploughman, Ploughman, where's your belt? / Lost it in the marsh where the glass is felt."]
PELL: That's new. Four days old, that one. They had it off the pan children.
KEEPER: Who made it up?
PELL: Nobody makes those up. They just turn up one morning, like gulls.
SERA: It's wrong. The Belt isn't in the marsh. It's four degrees east of where it was.
PELL: It rhymes, though, Surveyor. You'd never get "four degrees" to rhyme.
[A bell somewhere strikes ten. On the stroke, the door at the back opens. Ivo Thane walks the length of the room with a green-bound minute book under his arm. The benches stand.]
IVO: Surveyor.
[Sera looks at her paper. She doesn't answer.]
TAM: She does that.
PELL: Every time. You could set a clock by it.
IVO: Keeper. You are on time. The chair notes it.
[He sits in the middle chair, opens the minute book, and uncaps his pen.]
IVO: Sit, please. The harbor council is in session.
[Skip summary: The council gathers in the customs house. Tam is nervous about telling the seats what he saw. Sera has a map with five bearings and tells the keeper to give bearing and hour. Ivo arrives on the stroke of ten; Sera won't answer him.]

### Scene 3 — The session
[Objective: Hear item four.]
IVO: Items one to three are minuted. Harbor dues, the north slip, the price of salt.
IVO: Item four. Reports of tall figures in the north marsh.
IVO: The chair has five such reports this week. The harbormaster asked for the item. Harbormaster.
HARBORMASTER: I'll be quick. My men won't cross the pans after dusk. That's two boats not unloaded.
HARBORMASTER: I'd like to know if they're right not to. Tam Rook. You first.
[Tam stands. He holds his netting needle in both hands like a cap.]
TAM: I was mending on the loft roof. Noon. Sun out.
TAM: There was one stood in the reeds, past the last pan. Tall as the loft. Thin as a mast.
TAM: It didn't do anything. That was the worst bit. I mended a whole row and it didn't do anything.
CHANDLER: How far off?
TAM: Far enough I couldn't see its face.
TAM: Near enough I could see it hadn't got one.
[A murmur goes along the benches.]
COOPER: Was it fog, lad? Marsh fog stands up tall, of an autumn.
TAM: Couldn't say. I've never had fog in my lap.
[Laughter. Tam goes red and sits down.]
HARBORMASTER: Keeper. You're up all night with the best view in Saltreach. Seen anything stood in the marsh?
[Choice a: "Yes. Tall, and standing still."]
[Choice b: "I keep the light. I watch the sea."]
[Choices converge.]
IVO: The keeper's answer is noted.
[He writes one line in the minute book. It takes him exactly as long as every other line.]
IVO: The surveyor has asked to speak. The chair allows it.
[Sera steps up and unrolls the paper on the table. It is the north marsh, drawn exact, with five small crosses and five ruled lines.]
SERA: I'm speaking to the seats.
IVO: The surveyor will address the chair.
SERA: Harbormaster. Tell the chair I'm speaking to the seats.
HARBORMASTER: She's speaking to the seats, Magistrate.
IVO: So the chair hears. Go on.
SERA: Five reports. I took a bearing on every one, from where the witness stood.
SERA: Five lines. They cross in the north reeds, inside a quarter mile.
SERA: Two seen at dusk. One at dawn. Two at noon, in full sun.
SERA: Fog doesn't stand in one place for a week. Fog doesn't stand up at noon.
CHANDLER: What do you say they are, then?
SERA: I say they're there. I call them Plumbs. Tall, thin, dead straight.
COOPER: Plumbs! She's gone and named them.
SERA: I named them so they'd fit on paper. Things on paper get looked at.
SERA: I'm asking three things.
SERA: One. Minute them as seen. Bearing and hour. Not as weather.
SERA: Two. A lamp and a watch on the marsh road at dusk, until they're gone.
SERA: Three. Nobody on the marsh after dark. Not cutters, not pan folk, not children.
[She steps back from the table. She has not once looked at the middle chair.]
IVO: Thank you, Surveyor. The chair will answer the second point first, since it costs money.
IVO: A lamp on the marsh road burns a gill an hour. The harbor fund holds oil for eleven weeks.
IVO: All of it goes to the breakwater. The keeper's lamp brings the boats in.
IVO: A marsh lamp brings nothing in. It tells every boat that passes that Saltreach is afraid of its own reeds.
CHANDLER: He's right about the oil. I've the figures here.
CHANDLER: And it's not just the oil. Word gets down the coast we've got things stood in the reeds.
CHANDLER: The buyers stop coming for the herring we haven't got. And for the salt we have.
HARBORMASTER: My boy's out on those pans most days. Cutting with his uncle.
HARBORMASTER: I'd sleep easier with a lamp on that road. Whatever it costs.
IVO: The harbormaster would sleep easier. The chair does not doubt it.
IVO: The chair asks what the harbormaster would sell, in February, to buy the oil.
[The harbormaster looks at the table, and says nothing.]
PELL: Haze doesn't cut rope.
IVO: The benches do not speak in session, Mr Pell.
PELL: Didn't speak. Muttered. There's a difference, at my age.
IVO: Then the chair did not hear it.
IVO: We had a bad year. Two families sold up and went.
IVO: The fish buyers who still come here come because we are dull.
IVO: I would like us to stay dull. Dull is eleven weeks of oil. Frightened is a harbor of boats for sale.
HARBORMASTER: And the first point? Minute what folk saw?
IVO: The chair will minute what folk saw. The question is what to call it.
COOPER: My grandad had a word for it. When the cutters came in off the marsh, seeing men in the reeds.
COOPER: Fog-madness. The marsh fog gets in behind your eyes, and you see what's not there. Tall men, mostly.
COOPER: Rest, a hot dinner, keep off the pans after dark. It passes by spring.
IVO: Thank you. That is the word.
[Sera turns, for the first time, toward the middle chair. Then she turns back to the seats.]
SERA: Tam Rook saw it at noon, sober, in sun. His bearing crosses mine.
SERA: Pell's pots crossed mine. The keeper's eyes crossed mine. Three counts, one night.
SERA: How many counts makes it not madness?
[Nobody at the table answers. On the second bench, Pell says something into his cap.]
IVO: Those in favor of minuting item four as fog-madness, with no further action.
[Hands rise along the table. The harbormaster's comes up last, and slowly.]
IVO: Six. The chair does not vote. Carried.
[He writes. Then he reads it aloud, in the same voice he used for the price of salt.]
IVO: "Item four. Reports of tall figures in the north marsh. The council finds them consistent with fog-madness."
IVO: "Residents are advised to rest, eat hot food, and keep off the pans after dark. No further action."
SERA: Harbormaster. He's given them my third point.
IVO: Keep off the pans after dark. Yes. The chair agrees with the surveyor about the dark.
IVO: It disagrees about the reason.
[Sera rolls up the map, carefully, edge to edge.]
SERA: Harbormaster. Put in the minutes that the surveyor will come back.
HARBORMASTER: Magistrate?
IVO: Minuted.
[He writes one more line and closes the book without looking up. Sera walks out down the middle of the room. The benches lean back to let her pass.]

### Scene 5 — The smokehouse, noon

### Scene 6 — The north marsh
[The north marsh in afternoon sun. The reeds all lean one way, though there is no wind. Far out past the last salt pan, something tall and thin stands among them, very still.]
[Sera stands on a channel bank with the sextant up. The reed post beside her is covered in small chalk numbers.]

SERA: Keeper. Stand there. Left of the post. Don't block the line.
KEEPER: Hester sent herring.

### Scene 8 — The lamp room, the questions

### Scene 9 — The lamp room, the wrong question

### Scene 10 — The stair
[The spiral stair, one turn below the lamp-room door. Bram sits on the ninth step with an oil can and a rag. He heard the bolt.]
[Objective: Go down to the breakwater.]
BRAM: Ah. That's the bolt. I know that bolt. We've met.
BRAM: Sit a minute. Ninth step's oiled. It's having a better day than you are.
BRAM: How long since you slept? Don't tell me. I can see it. You've got night-shift eyes.
KEEPER: I haven't, since the lamp went out this morning.
BRAM: Council, marsh, Plumbs, Sera and a bolt. On no sleep. That's a whole season in one day.
KEEPER: I asked why it matters what the council writes down.
BRAM: Oh, Keeper.
BRAM: Well. You asked her the one question she's been asking herself every dusk. She doesn't like it any better from you.
KEEPER: She bolted it.
BRAM: So. Funny thing about that bolt. She bolted it on me, the first night.
BRAM: I come up the stair to oil the hinges, and there's a woman in my lamp room, chalking my wall.
BRAM: I said, "Have you eaten?" That's all. Bolt.
BRAM: So I left the pot on the step. Came back in the morning, it was still full. Cold as a fish.
KEEPER: And the next night?
BRAM: Pot on the step again. Came back up at midnight, and it was empty. Not a word. Empty pot, and the bolt.
BRAM: Then the door opens. She doesn't look round. She says, "Hold this."
BRAM: Hands me the sextant, so she can chalk with both hands. That was it. That was the whole welcome.
BRAM: Never asked her a thing, after. Just held what she gave me.
KEEPER: Why does the council matter so much to her?
[Bram turns the oil can in his hands for a while.]
BRAM: She stood up in that customs house on her own. First time. The day the Belt slipped.
BRAM: Whole town on the benches. Ivo in the middle chair. I was out hauling nets.
BRAM: Nobody stood up with her. Not one. Then he said "haze," and the whole room was glad to hear it.
BRAM: She came up here that night with a stick of chalk. Been up here every dusk since.

KEEPER: She shut me out.
BRAM: She shut the door. You're still on the stair. That's different.

WREN: I am! I'm the best at stairs! Is there a prize?

WREN: I'm quiet! This is quiet! Is this quiet?

BRAM: I've half a boat to unload before dark, and Hester's pot to fetch.
BRAM: Go on. Light your lamp. It took me supper to get in.
BRAM: What've you got?
KEEPER: A lamp.
BRAM: Well. Lamps are a start. Ask my family.
[Objective: Walk down to the harbor.]

### Scene 11 — The smokehouse step, toward dusk
[The harbor road in the last hour of light. Hester sits on the smokehouse step with her book open on her knee, banking smoke drifting out of the vents behind her. The minute is still pinned on the customs house board across the road.]
[Objective: Walk to the breakwater.]
HESTER: Keeper. Did she eat?
KEEPER: Half.
HESTER: Half! Hold still, I want that in big letters.
[She writes it, slowly, with her tongue between her teeth.]
HESTER: "Sera Vant. Half a herring. Marsh." That's the first half I've had off her since it started.
HESTER: Bram'll be sick with jealousy. Don't tell him. Yes, tell him. I want to see his face.
HESTER: Now. You've a face like a shut door. Who bolted it?
KEEPER: How did you know?
HESTER: I've fed this harbor thirty years. I know a bolted face. Out with it.
KEEPER: I asked her why it matters what the council writes down.
HESTER: Oh, you daft sprat.
HESTER: That's like asking Pell why it matters where his pots are. It's not a question. It's a kick.
KEEPER: I didn't mean it as one.
HESTER: Nobody ever does. That's why it's on the slate and not in the fire.
[She wipes her hands on her apron, twice, though there's nothing on them.]
HESTER: Here's how it is, in Saltreach. Nobody asks for anything.
HESTER: Sera Vant's never asked me for a crumb. Bram's never asked me to stop feeding her on his tab.
HESTER: That Flint boy sat on my bench a week and never asked once. I had to put the plate in his hands.
KEEPER: And you?
HESTER: Me? I'm the one who doesn't need asking. That's my job. Somebody's got to have it.
KEEPER: Jory sits on the breakwater all night.
HESTER: Does he. On the bollard at the end? Facing the lamp?
KEEPER: Yes.
HESTER: Then let him. That's the only thing he's asked this town for since he got off the packet, and he didn't even ask.
KEEPER: The story at noon. About his father's book.
HESTER: Who's asking?
KEEPER: Me.
HESTER: Then here's what I know, Keeper. I don't write secrets down. Only debts.
HESTER: And a story told at the slip is neither. It's just weather.
[Across the road the minute flaps on its nail in the evening wind. She looks at it, and back at her book.]
HESTER: Ivo Thane eats here Thursdays. Sits by the door. Pays in coin, every time. Only man in Saltreach who does.
KEEPER: Is that bad?
HESTER: It's not bad. It's not friendly, either. A man who pays cash doesn't want to be in anybody's book.
HESTER: Fog-madness. In ink. And there's my book with "half a herring" in it.
HESTER: Mine's the truer of the two, and nobody'll ever read it out in session.
HESTER: Go on. Light's going. Mind you keep yours lit.
[Objective: Light the breakwater lamp at dusk.]
[Skip summary: On her way to the breakwater, the keeper tells Hester that Sera ate half the herring, and Hester writes it down. ### Scene 12 — The breakwater, dusk
[The breakwater at dusk. The Keeper lights the lamp. The wick is cut square, and the flame stands up straight and yellow.]
[At the far end, Jory sits on the same bollard as at dawn.]
[Objective: Light the lamp. Write the dusk in the logbook.]
JORY: Lit on time. Not that I looked.
KEEPER: You're back.
JORY: It's a free wall. I said.
JORY: You squared the wick.
KEEPER: You told me to.
JORY: I said it wasn't my business. That's different.
[He stands, walks to the parapet beside the lamp-house door, and runs his thumb along the top stone until it stops.]
JORY: Come here a minute. Don't make a thing of it.
[In the parapet's top stone there is a narrow V, cut by hand, old and weathered smooth.]
JORY: The old keeper cut that. The year the light went up.
JORY: Put your eye down to it at dusk. The old tower sits in the bottom of the V.
JORY: The Belt used to sit right on top of the tower. Like a cap on a post. Every clear dusk.
JORY: He'd check it. Not for anything. Just to see it was there. Then he'd write "Belt on the tower" in the book.
KEEPER: Every night?
JORY: Every clear night. Ten years. Neat as you like.
[He hears himself say it. He doesn't take it back.]
[The Keeper puts an eye to the notch. The old tower stands dark in the V. Three stars hang above it, but not on top of it. East of it.]
KEEPER: It's not on the tower.
JORY: No. It's a finger off. East. It's been a finger off every night since I got in.
JORY: I thought it was me. Thought I'd come back and the place didn't fit any more.
KEEPER: Does Sera know about this notch?
JORY: Nobody knows. Nobody looks at the breakwater. That's what it's for.
JORY: People look at the old tower. They look at the sky. They look at the boats.
JORY: Nobody looks at the light that brings the boats in. Not till it isn't lit.
KEEPER: You do.
JORY: I'm sat right next to it. It's hard not to.
JORY: Tam says I come out here to see if you'll fail. He's wrong.
KEEPER: Why, then?
JORY: Because it's lit. That's all. Somebody lit it. It's nice, watching something get done right.
KEEPER: You could come in. Out of the wind. There's the stool.
JORY: No.
JORY: That's the keeper's stool. I'm not the keeper. Everybody in this town's very clear on that.
JORY: The bollard's mine. Nobody's ever wanted it. That's the best kind of thing to have.
KEEPER: Can I write it down?
JORY: Why are you asking me?
KEEPER: It's his notch.
JORY: It's the council's wall. He cut it on the council's time. Everything out here's the council's. I told you.
JORY: It's your book now. Write what you want in it.
JORY: Just don't put my name next to it. Flint's a bad name to have on a wall.
[He takes something out of his coat pocket and turns it over in his fingers. It is half a packet-boat ticket, folded soft.]
KEEPER: What's that?
JORY: Return half. Packet ticket. You buy them both at once, down the coast. It's cheaper.
KEEPER: You haven't used it.
JORY: It doesn't go off. It's good till the packet sinks.
JORY: Which, with the tides doing what Pell says, might be Thursday. Not my business.
KEEPER: Why did you come back?
[Jory looks at the lamp.]
JORY: Your lamp's drawing well. Hear that? That's a good flame.
KEEPER: That's not what I asked.
JORY: No.
[The Keeper writes in the logbook: "Dusk. Clear. Belt one finger east of the tower, by the notch."]
[Jory reads it upside down.]
JORY: That's how he'd have put it. More or less. He'd have spelled "notch" wrong.
JORY: Go on. She'll be at her wall by now.
JORY: The lamp'll burn till dawn whether you watch it or not.
[He says it like a thing he has heard said before. He sits back down on the bollard, facing the lamp.]
[Objective: Take the dusk count to the lamp room.]
[Skip summary: At dusk Jory is back on his bollard. He lets the keeper write it in the log, but asks to be kept off the wall, and still won't say why he came back.]

### Scene 14 — The lamp room, supper
[Bram comes up the last steps with Hester's pot under one arm and three bowls in the other hand. He sees the stub of chalk in the Keeper's hand and stops on the top step.]
[Objective: Eat. Then back to the breakwater.]
BRAM: Well.
BRAM: Well, well. Door's open. Chalk's out. And I've only brought three bowls.
SERA: Wren can share.
WREN: Share what? Is it supper? What's in it? Is it the fish with the faces?
BRAM: Herring stew. Hester's. On my slate, with the rest of the harbor.
WREN: Keeper's got a chalk! Did you get a chalk? Can I have a chalk? I won't lick it. Much.
SERA: No.
WREN: When I'm bigger?
[Bram looks at Wren, at the four rolls in her sleeves, and ladles stew.]
BRAM: When you're bigger, love. Eat your fish first.
[Sera takes a bowl. She eats two spoons, standing at the glass.]
BRAM: Two spoons. Keeper, you saw that. I'll want a witness. Hester won't believe me.
SERA: Don't write it in the council's book.
BRAM: Wouldn't dream of it. They'd minute it as fog-madness.
WREN: Bram! I did a test! I passed, or I went past it. Sera didn't say which.
BRAM: Did you, now. Was she gentle?
WREN: She was exact! Is that the same?
BRAM: Not quite, love. Not quite. But it's what she's got.
SERA: Hester says it's fog, then.
BRAM: Hester says it's fog in the street and Plumbs in the kitchen, and the children in by dark.
SERA: Hester was faster than the minute.
BRAM: Hester's faster than the tide. Someone should put her on the council.
SERA: She'd never sit. She'd have to stop feeding people to do it.
WREN: Keeper, what did you mark? Was it the sky? Is the sky on the wall now?
KEEPER: A bit of it.
WREN: Which bit? Is it my bit? Do I have a bit?
SERA: Everyone in this room's got a bit. Eat.
WREN: Bram's got a bit? Where's Bram's bit?
BRAM: My bit's the pot, love. Every night. Nobody chalks the pot.
[Sera doesn't answer that. She looks at her bowl a moment longer than she needs to.]
BRAM: Keeper, you'll want to be back on that breakwater before Thane minutes you.
BRAM: Take the end of the stew. Lamps burn better with a keeper that's eaten.
KEEPER: Is that true?
BRAM: No. But my grandad said it, and he kept this light longer than I've been alive. I'm not arguing with him now.
WREN: Keeper, are you going? To the other light? The squashed one?

WREN: Will you look up here? From there? I'll wave. You won't see it. But I'll have waved.
BRAM: That's Hester talking. She's been teaching you her book.

SERA: It's not on the pans. It's at the crater.
BRAM: Nobody goes out there. Not after today. Not with the whole town minuted mad.
SERA: Somebody does.
[Wren comes to the glass in the big coat and looks out at the little moving light.]
WREN: Is it somebody looking for me?
[Bram puts his bowl down. He doesn't answer.]
SERA: Keeper. Bearing and hour. Mark it.
[The Keeper raises the stub of chalk and marks the wall. Far out on the marsh, the small lamp goes out.]
