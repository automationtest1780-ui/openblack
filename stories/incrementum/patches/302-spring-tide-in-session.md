## Planning sheet (gacha 14.2)

**Player fantasy:** the sea comes into the council room in the middle of the session, and you watch the most careful man in Saltreach stand up to his knees in it and tell the truth anyway, at the one moment he always knew it would cost the most.

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**
- **Ivo** wants to read the black book to the council, at a session he called for it, in an orderly room. Obstacle: the spring tide comes hours early and floods the room before he opens it, the town crowds in shouting, and every seat, Bram included, offers him a reason to adjourn. Cost: he reads it anyway, standing in the water, at the worst time. The town learns that he knew, that he called it haze knowing, and that he let Old Flint's name carry the bad year. He takes his own notices off the door.
- **Sera** wants the council to post a tide, now that she has the keeper's readings and the tables are dead. Obstacle: she still won't speak to the chair, and the water beats her figure by a quarter of an hour. Cost: she hears that her first count was not the first, that her tables were wrong a year ago in someone else's book, and that Bram knew before the session. She says nothing after the reading.
- **Bram** wants to tell Sera himself before two o'clock. Obstacle: she won't hear him ("Tell him after"), and the water comes first. Cost: he holds the light for Ivo's reading, steady, and Sera sees that he was not surprised.
- **Jory** (bench) wants the light's book to stand as honest. He brings it to council because Sera asks. Cost and gain at once: his father is cleared in front of the whole harbor, by a man who had the line for a year. He walks out without a word.
- **Nell** (bench) wants a tide and a date. She gets the reason for the bad year, and takes it as a reason to go: "The thirtieth, first light."

## Script

### Scene 1 — The breakwater, dawn
[Day 25. Dawn on the breakwater. The lamp still burns, pale in the grey. The water is already high on the stones at an hour no table in Saltreach allows.]
[Jory sits on the step of the lamp housing with the breakwater log on his knees. Sera comes down the breakwater with her tide book under her arm. On the back of her hand, in chalk: 16.]
[Objective: Read the log with Sera.]
JORY: Keeper. Lamp's lit. Not that I looked.
JORY: Surveyor. On the breakwater. That's new.
SERA: The book comes up the stair every morning now. Today I came down for it.
SERA: Springs. I want the stick as well as the book. Give it here.
JORY: It's the keeper's book.
SERA: Then the keeper can give it here.
[Choice a: "Here."]
[Choice b: "Jory's been minding it."]
[Choices converge.]
JORY: Go on. Don't make a thing of it.
[Jory holds the log out. Sera kneels on the wet stone with it and runs a finger down the last page.]
SERA: Dusk. Three hours twenty ahead.
SERA: Three o'clock. Three hours fifty.
SERA: Thirty minutes in a night. It jumped at the shift. Your hand says so. "Water up the stick at midnight."
KEEPER: It slapped the stick. Same as before.
SERA: Same as the bar night. Write that every time. Even when it's the same.
JORY: You read it standing up. On wet stone.
SERA: It's quicker standing.
JORY: The old keeper read it sitting. Every line, with his finger. He said a log's read like a letter, not a bill.
SERA: What's the difference?
JORY: A bill you check. A letter you believe.
SERA: I check everything.
JORY: I know. That's why I give it you.
[She opens her own tide book beside it, at the printed table. Every column is struck through in pencil.]
SERA: Springs today. Biggest water of the month.
SERA: The table says high water at twenty past six tonight.
SERA: Less four hours, by your book. Twenty past two. Call it half past.
SERA: Springs on top. Over the quay by a foot. More, if the wind backs.
JORY: Council sits at two. Notice went up on the door last night.
JORY: "Item: a report from the chair." Whole harbor's talking about it. He's never reported anything.
SERA: He'll be sitting in it, then. At half past two. Up to his boots.
JORY: Will you tell him?
SERA: I'll tell the harbormaster.
JORY: Same room.
SERA: Different man.
[Jory almost laughs, and stops himself. He looks at the lamp, as if checking it's lit.]
JORY: Sixteen on your hand. Corra'll want that. For her door.
SERA: Tell her four from twenty. Tell her it was midnight.
JORY: I'm not telling anybody anything. I mind a lamp.
SERA: You told the keeper about the stub. You told me about the stitching.
JORY: That was the book. The book's different. Books ought to be told about.
SERA: Then tell Corra, from the book. Sixteen. Midnight.
JORY: ...Fine. From the book.
SERA: Then mind this. Bring the book to council. Two o'clock.
JORY: The light's book? In the customs house?
SERA: I want the seats to see whose hand it's in. Numbers in a log, every night, kept honest.
SERA: They believe a book. They don't believe me.
JORY: They didn't believe the last book that came out of this lamp house.
SERA: Then bring this one. It's got a stub in it. The stub's honest. You said.
JORY: Two days back I took it in to be signed a page short. Magistrate signed it. Didn't ask whose knife.
SERA: He wouldn't.
JORY: Didn't even look up. Just signed. Like he'd been expecting it.
SERA: He expects everything. It's how he gets out of it.
[Jory looks at the log for a long moment. He closes it.]
JORY: Not my business.
JORY: ...I'll carry it. Somebody ought to. Not that it's my book.
SERA: Keeper. Bram's been at the tower since six. With bread.
KEEPER: He wanted to talk to you.
SERA: He can talk at supper. It's cold either way.
KEEPER: He said it was important.
SERA: Everything he carries is important. He carried a page five days.
SERA: Half past two is important. That's what I'm carrying today.
SERA: Go up and get your bread. Then two o'clock. Your chair's the one by the door.
[Skip summary: At dawn on day 25, Sera reads the keeper's breakwater log, as she does every morning now. The tide jumped at last night's shift, and today is a spring tide: by her sums it will come at half past two, hours ahead of the table, and over the quay. The council sits at two for "a report from the chair". She asks Jory to bring the log to council, and puts Bram off until supper.]

### Scene 2 — The lamp room, morning
[The lamp room in morning light. The chalk wall full, floor to ceiling. Bram sits on the top step tearing a loaf into a cloth. Wren stands at the glass in Bram's coat. The sleeves end halfway up her forearms now.]
[In the shadow of the stairwell behind her, where the light doesn't reach, Wren glows. Very faintly. Like a coal under ash.]
[Objective: Take Bram's bread down.]
BRAM: Keeper. You've seen her, then. On the breakwater.
KEEPER: She's going to council.
BRAM: Course she is. With her tide.
BRAM: That's good, that is. That's her in the right room for once.
[He tears the loaf again. It's already in more pieces than anyone needs.]
KEEPER: Did you tell her?
BRAM: No.
BRAM: I asked him for a day. A day to tell her myself. He gave it me.
BRAM: I had it. It went past me on the stair.
BRAM: Twice last night I got as far as "Sera." Then the shift came. Then I got as far as "Sera" again.
BRAM: Then I made the tea.
WREN: He made very good tea. I noticed.
WREN: Bram's been doing his quiet all morning. Only with words in. Is that a new kind?
BRAM: It's the old kind, Sparrow. With the lid off.
WREN: Keeper. Look in the corner by the stair. Where the sun doesn't go.
WREN: I'm a bit lit there. Just at the edges. Since the shift.
WREN: Bram says it's the coat. The coat's never been lit in its life.
BRAM: It's a very old coat. It's had a lot of suppers. Some of them were bright.
[Wren turns from the glass. She speaks more slowly than she used to, and looks at the keeper a beat before she answers anything.]
WREN: Keeper. Is it true there's a council today? A special one?
KEEPER: Two o'clock.
WREN: Can I come down? I'd stand at the back. I'm tall enough for the back now.
BRAM: No.
WREN: Because of Tam's hands.
BRAM: Because it's an up-here day.
WREN: It's been up-here days for a week. The harbor looks very far down today.
WREN: Further than the stairs. Like looking at it from a long way up.
WREN: Since last night I keep remembering little bits. Not whole things. Just cold. And wide.
WREN: Like a field with no hedges. Is that a memory, Bram? Or just the window?
BRAM: It's the window, love. Windows do that. Ask my grandad.
WREN: Your grandad's in the churchyard.
BRAM: Then ask the window.
[Wren smiles. Then she looks up at the glass a moment too long, and turns back.]
WREN: Will Ivo be at the special council?
BRAM: He called it.
WREN: Then tell him something from me. Tell him the sky's sixteen.
WREN: Tell him sixteen's a tidy number. It halves all the way down.
WREN: See if his face does anything. Tell me after. Exactly what it did.
BRAM: I'll watch his face for you.
WREN: You always watch faces. You're the best at faces.
[Bram folds the cloth over the bread. He doesn't get up.]
BRAM: Keeper. Two o'clock, he reads it. To the seats. To the benches. To her.
BRAM: She'll hear it in a room full of people. From him.
BRAM: She deserves to hear it from somebody who'd stand next to her after.
[Choice a: "Then tell her on the way down."]
[Choice b: "You could still be that."]
[Choices converge.]
BRAM: On the road. Aye. Straight. No soup. No funny thing about anything.
BRAM: Straight's your job. I'll borrow it. Just the once.
BRAM: Listen. Tell me if it's straight. I'll say it to you first.
[He stands up, faces the empty doorway as if Sera were in it, and takes a breath.]
BRAM: "Sera. Ivo's got a book."
BRAM: ...That's straight, is it?
KEEPER: It's a start.
BRAM: "He's had it years. He saw it first. Before you."
BRAM: No. She'll hear "before you" and stop listening. She'll be off counting the days.
BRAM: "Sera. Funny thing about Ivo—" No. That's sideways. That's me.
BRAM: "Sera. I've known since last night, and I'm telling you before he does, because—"
[He stops.]
BRAM: Because I'm a coward with good timing. That's the end of that sentence. Every way I try it.
KEEPER: Because you want to be next to her when she hears it.
BRAM: ...Aye. That one. I'll use that one. Don't tell her it was yours.
WREN: What's he going to read? Is it a story?
BRAM: It's numbers, Sparrow. Mostly.
WREN: Sera's numbers?
BRAM: ...Somebody's.
WREN: You said "somebody's" like it was a heavy word. Is it heavy?
BRAM: Heavier than bread. Here. Stay up here. Count the boats for me.
WREN: There's none out. There's none out every day now. Is that a count?
BRAM: It's the shortest one there is.
[He goes down with the bread. Wren watches the stair after him, glowing faintly where the light doesn't reach.]
[Skip summary: In the lamp room, Bram tells the keeper he still hasn't told Sera about Ivo's ledger, though Ivo reads it at two o'clock. Wren, brighter since last night's shift and slower-spoken, says she keeps remembering little bits of something cold and wide. She sends Ivo a message: the sky is sixteen. Bram resolves to tell Sera straight on the road down.]

### Scene 3 — The smokehouse, noon
[Hester's smokehouse at noon. The benches are full and loud. Tam sits by the fire with a spoon wedged in his bandaged hand. Nell stands at the end of a bench with her pipe, unlit.]
[Objective: Eat before the session.]
HESTER: Keeper. Sit. You've a face like a herring that's heard about the council.
TAM: Keeper! Look. Spoon. On my own. Hester says it's showing off.

TAM: The dog didn't mind. The dog's never minded anything.

### Scene 5 — The session
[Objective: Hear the items.]

### Scene 6 — The water

### Scene 7 — The flood fight
[The council room, knee-deep and rising. Benches on end against the wall, children on the table, the crowd packed behind Bram's light. The Tide-Plumbs stand in the doorway and in the road beyond it, every faceless head toward the room.]
