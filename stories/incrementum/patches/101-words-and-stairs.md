## Planning sheet (gacha 14.2)

**Player fantasy:** the morning after a miracle, you learn a town's stairs and its people, and the impossible turns up at noon and stands in the reeds.

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**
- **Bram** wants Wren down the stair and back up it in one piece, and everybody home for supper. Obstacle: the marsh he walks every night now has tall things standing in it. Cost: he opens his family's crate in Tam's loft and gives the old tower's gear away to be used up.
- **Wren** wants to know what everything is and to belong to someone on the ground. Obstacle: the town stares, the stair won't give her the same number twice, and the tall things are frightening in a way she can't name. Cost: she learns the word "careful", and she gets written into Hester's book.
- **Nell** wants to take the fleet south before the gales. Obstacle: *Patience* is sprung on a rock that isn't there. Cost: her boathook is what wakes the figure in the reeds.
- **Tam** wants to keep mending. Obstacle: he's the one high enough to see it. Cost: none yet. He keeps mending.
- **Sera** wants the number before anyone calls it weather. Obstacle: she can't be at the slips and the marsh at once. Cost: she has to trust the keeper's book for the positions.

## Script

### Scene 1 — The lamp room, morning
[Full morning on the salt-stained glass. The visitor who climbed the stair at dawn has gone back down to the harbor. Wren kneels at the window in Bram's coat, sleeves rolled four times, nose against the pane.]
[Objective: Get Wren down the stair.]
BRAM: Morning, all. That's the lamp room in daylight. It doesn't improve much.
BRAM: Today's job's a small one. Get this one down the stair without anybody falling off it.
WREN: Bram! The dark went away! Where did it go? Did somebody put it in a box?
BRAM: That's morning, love. Comes every day. You'll get sick of it.
WREN: Morning. Is it a kind of fire? It's on everything. It's on the sea!
WREN: Is the sea always that wide? Is it full up, or is there more coming?
BRAM: There's always more coming. That's the trouble with the sea.
[Sera stands at the chalk wall with her coat already on, reading last night's three marks.]
SERA: Tide turns at ten past nine. I'm at the slips till noon. The tables want checking.
KEEPER: After last night?
SERA: Because of last night. If the water's off, I want it in numbers before anyone says weather.
BRAM: You'll eat something on the way.
SERA: Hester will make me. That counts.
BRAM: It does not count. Being made to doesn't count.
SERA: It counts in Saltreach. You told me so.
BRAM: I told you that about Hester's book. Not about breakfast.
SERA: Aren't you hauling for Garrow today?
BRAM: Told her last night I'm off the boats. She'll say I've gone soft.
SERA: You have gone soft. Since the first night. I counted.
BRAM: You did not.
SERA: No. But it's true.
WREN: Sera! Where are you going? Can I come? Can I count the water too?
SERA: No. You stay with Bram.
WREN: Will you come back? Do people come back, after they go down?
SERA: At dusk. Every dusk. I haven't missed one.
WREN: Every dusk! Is that a lot of dusks? Is it more than twelve?
SERA: It's exactly as many as there have been.
WREN: Up here is very up. Is down nice? Is there more down than up?
SERA: There's a hundred and eight steps of down. Bram knows every one.
[She stops at the head of the stair and looks back at the Keeper.]
SERA: You saw it fall. Don't let anybody talk you out of the numbers.
KEEPER: Which numbers?
SERA: Twelve degrees. And four. And three counts. You were there for all of them.
SERA: The magistrate will have a word for last night by dinner. It won't be a number.
[She goes. Her steps go round and round, down and away.]
WREN: She went round and round. Is that how down works? Does it always go round?
BRAM: In a lighthouse it does. Saves room.
WREN: Can I do it? Can I do down now? Is it hard? Is it a game?
BRAM: It's a stair. Old one. I'll teach you.
BRAM: Keeper, you'll want your bed. Your first proper week of nights starts tonight.
KEEPER: I'll walk down with you.
BRAM: Good. Two of us and one of her. That's decent odds on a stair.
WREN: What's odds? Is it a kind of fish?
BRAM: Ask the magistrate. He collects them.
WREN: The cross man? Will he come back? I nearly made him smile. I'm going to do it properly next time.
BRAM: You'll want a running start, love. And careful on the top step. Come on.
WREN: Keeper, did you sleep? Do keepers sleep? Where does your light go in the day?
KEEPER: It sleeps too. Till dusk.
WREN: Lights sleep! Is everything allowed to sleep? Even the sea?
BRAM: Not the sea. The sea's never slept a wink. That's why it's so cross.
WREN: Do I hold your hand? Which one? I've got two. Do you want the one with more sleeve?
BRAM: That one. It's warmer. Careful, now.
[Skip summary: The morning after the fall, Sera leaves to check her tide tables at the slips. Bram stays home from the boats to teach Wren the lighthouse stair, and the keeper, due on night shift, goes down with them.]

### Scene 2 — The old lighthouse stair
[The spiral stair, salt-worn. Bram goes down backwards, one step below Wren, his storm lantern hooked on his belt. The Keeper follows above.]
[Objective: Teach Wren the stair.]
BRAM: Right. Lighthouse stair, lesson one. My dad gave me this lesson, so it's a good one.
BRAM: Rule one. You hold the wall. Wall's your friend.
WREN: Hello, wall! Is it cold because it's sad, or because it's a wall?
BRAM: It's cold because it's a wall. Rule two. One foot goes down, then the other foot comes and meets it.
WREN: Meets it? Do feet say hello?
BRAM: Every step. Polite things, feet.
[Wren puts one bare foot down. Then the other. She looks enormously pleased with herself.]
WREN: One! Is that one? I did a one!
BRAM: That's one. Hundred and seven to go.
WREN: Two! Three! Are they all the same tall? What if one's taller and nobody told me?
BRAM: Then you find out with your toes, and you yell, and I catch you.
WREN: Four! Five! Bram, what's the word for holding on so a thing doesn't go away?
BRAM: Holding?
WREN: No, the other one. The one you said to me at the top.
BRAM: Careful?
WREN: Careful! I'm being careful at the wall! Six! Seven! Eight!
BRAM: Now. Mind the ninth step. It's had a hard life.
WREN: Why? What happened to it? Did it fall too?
BRAM: My grandad dropped an anvil on it. Don't ask me why he had an anvil up a lighthouse.
WREN: Why did he have an anvil up a lighthouse?
BRAM: I said don't ask me.
[Wren steps over the chipped ninth step with enormous ceremony.]
WREN: Nine! Poor nine. I stepped very soft. Is it better now?
BRAM: Better than it's been in sixty years.
[Choice a: "Count with her."]
[Choice b: "Let her count on her own."]
[Choices converge.]
WREN: Ten! Eleven! Twelve! Twelve is mine. Sera said. Is every twelve mine, or just that one?
BRAM: Just the one, love. Other twelves belong to other people.
WREN: Thirteen. Fourteen. What's that hole? Is the wall broken?
BRAM: That's a window. Lets the light out.
KEEPER: Or in.
BRAM: Or in. Depends on the hour. Keeper's right.
WREN: Window. Win-dow. Is it a door for looking?
BRAM: That's exactly what it is.
WREN: Twenty! Twenty-one! Bram, did you do this when you were small?
BRAM: Every day. My dad went backwards below me, like this. So if I fell, I'd fall on him.
WREN: Did you fall on him?
BRAM: Twice. He said I was softer than the stair. He was lying.
WREN: Is that what dads do? Go backwards below you?
[Bram is quiet for one step.]
BRAM: That's one of the things, yes.
WREN: Thirty! Thirty-ten! Thirty-eleven!
BRAM: Forty. After thirty-nine comes forty.
WREN: Why? Who said? Did they ask the stair?
BRAM: Nobody asked the stair. That's the trouble with numbers.
[They reach a flat place where the stair widens. A slit window shows the harbor, small and bright below.]
WREN: Bram! The stair stopped! Is it tired? Is this the bottom?
BRAM: That's a landing. A flat bit, so the stair can catch its breath. You too.
WREN: Landing. Is that where birds stop? Wrens land. I land!
BRAM: You do. You're the best lander I've ever seen.
[Wren presses her face to the slit window. Below, the slips, boats, the smokehouse smoke, people already looking up at the tower.]
WREN: Why are they all looking up here? Is something wrong with the tower?
BRAM: Nothing wrong with it. They're just nosy. Saltreach is mostly nose.
KEEPER: Are they looking at her?
BRAM: Some of them. Half the harbor was up last night. They'll want a look.
BRAM: Let them look. Looking's free. Hester'll charge them for it if she can.
WREN: Can I wave at them? Is that allowed? Would they wave back?
BRAM: Try one. See what happens.
[Wren waves through the slit. Far below, one small figure on the slip waves back, uncertainly.]
WREN: They waved! Somebody waved! Bram, I'm a waver now!
BRAM: That'll be Pell. He's soft for anything that falls in the sea.
[They go on. Wren's counting echoes round the stone, faster and faster, then the door at the bottom, and daylight.]
WREN: Ninety-five! Ninety-six! Door! Is that all of it? Ninety-six is all of it?
BRAM: Hundred and eight, love. Always has been.
WREN: But I counted every one! I said every one out loud. Is the stair shorter going down?
BRAM: You skipped a few on the landing, I expect.
WREN: I didn't skip. I said them all, twice for nine because it was sad.
BRAM: So. Funny thing about stairs. They're a hundred and eight in the morning.
BRAM: And a hundred and eight at night. My grandad's number. My dad's. Mine.
KEEPER: Does it matter?
BRAM: No. Course not. She'll get it right going up.
[He doesn't look at the stair. He holds the door for her.]
[Skip summary: Bram teaches Wren the lighthouse stair the way his father taught him: hold the wall, mind the ninth step. She learns "careful", "window" and "landing", waves at the harbor, and counts ninety-six steps where Bram has always counted a hundred and eight.]

### Scene 3 — The headland path
[Outside the tower door: a steep path down the headland to the harbor, grass flattened by wind, the sea loud below. Wren stops on the doorstep and stares at everything at once.]
[Objective: Walk Wren down to the harbor.]
BRAM: Headland path. Harbor's at the bottom. We go down slow and we learn some words on the way.
WREN: More words? Do I need more? I've got careful and landing and window.
BRAM: You've got three. Pell's got about forty, and he gets by.
KEEPER: What's the first one?
BRAM: She picks. Point at something, love.
[Wren points straight up.]
WREN: That!
BRAM: That's sky. Next.
WREN: But what is it? Is it a ceiling? Does it have an upstairs?
BRAM: Next. Point at something down.
[She points at a gull hanging on the wind over the cliff edge.]
WREN: That! The white one, standing on nothing!
BRAM: Gull. Thief. Sits on your boat and eats your bait and looks at you like you owe it.
WREN: Gull. Is it allowed to stand on nothing? Can I stand on nothing?
BRAM: No. Gulls have a permit. Next.
[She points at the smoke rising from the long low roof by the slips.]
WREN: That! It's going up! Is it going home?
BRAM: Smoke. That's Hester's smokehouse. Where the fish go to get tasty.
WREN: Do the fish want to go?
BRAM: Nobody asks them. Same as numbers.
[Below them on the path, three children sit on a wall swinging their heels. They stop talking when they see Wren, then start again, louder, the way children do.]
[They chant a skipping rhyme at each other, slapping hands on each beat.]
WREN: What are they saying? Is it a word? Is it a long word?
KEEPER: It's a rhyme.
BRAM: "Ploughman, Ploughman, where's your belt? Lost it in the marsh where the glass is felt."
BRAM: That's new. That wasn't there yesterday. Give kids one night and they've a song.
WREN: Is it about me? I was in the glass. Am I in a song now?
BRAM: You're near a song. That's plenty for a first day.
WREN: Can I do the slapping? Will they let me?
[She runs the last few steps down to the wall and holds up both hands, sleeves and all. The children look at each other. The smallest one slaps her palms, once, quick, and they all scatter, shrieking with laughter.]
WREN: They ran! Is that the game? Did I win?
BRAM: You did all right. They'll be back to look at you again by dinner.
[Wren turns round and points back up the path, at the tall dark tower on the headland.]
WREN: That! What's that one called? The tall one we came out of?
BRAM: Lighthouse. The old one. My family's.
WREN: Is it a home? Is home a word? You said it at the top, I think.
