## Planning sheet (gacha 14.2)

**Player fantasy:** the girl you helped carry out of the crater is suddenly tall, quick and delighted with herself, and you're the one standing at the foot of the stair when the whole harbor finds out what that costs.

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**
- **Wren** wants to show everyone how big she is and learn something with her new hands (a mending knot from Tam). Obstacle: the harbor starts looking at her; her bones itch again on day 20; the sky moves while Tam is holding her hands. Cost: she burns the first person who taught her anything just because she asked. She grows in front of the whole harbor, and she stops letting herself glow. By night she turns her back on the glass.
- **Bram** wants her small a little longer and everyone home. Obstacle: she's a hand taller, and since the breakwater night he can't look the keeper in the eye. Cost: he takes the crater road without the keeper, leaves them to mind her, and isn't there when the light goes. He calls her "Sparrow" for the first time, and in the evening finds nothing on her sleeves to roll.
- **Tam** wants to teach her a knot. Obstacle: her hands get hot. Cost: he holds on, his palms burn, and he can't mend for weeks. He forgives her the same night and goes on teaching her knots through his bandages.
- **Hester** wants the harbor fed and kept calm. Obstacle: the harbor has started to be afraid of a girl on her own slate. Cost: she dresses Tam's hands with half the town in her door, and hears the saying she knows will outlast her soup.

## Script

### Scene 1 — The lamp room, morning
[Grey morning. Rain-dark glass. The chalk wall is full except for the bottom course by the door, where a new mark sits, small: 28.]
[SERA sits against the wall with her tide book on her knees. Every column on the open page is struck through. Under the window, a heap of coat stirs.]
[Objective: See what's changed.]
WREN: Sera? Sera, is it morning? It smells like morning.
SERA: It's morning.
WREN: Did I miss something? Your face is the tired kind.
SERA: Two things.
WREN: Two! Which two? Good ones?
SERA: Last night the tide came in two hours and ten minutes early.
SERA: Then at two in the morning, the Belt moved. Thirty-two to twenty-eight.
WREN: Without me? I slept through the whole sky?
[WREN sits up. The coat falls off her shoulders. Her legs come out from under it, a long way.]
[She stands. The hem that brushed her ankles hangs at her shins. Her head is past the third bar of the window frame, nearly at the fourth.]
WREN: Sera. Sera, look. The window's gone short.
SERA: The window's the size it was.
WREN: Then I'm the size I wasn't! Look at my hands! Look how far away they are!
[She holds them up. Both come out of the sleeves to the wrist. She laughs. Her laugh is lower than it was.]
WREN: I can reach the top bar. Look. I'm touching the top bar!
SERA: Stand still.
[SERA looks at her the way she looks at the Belt. Her hand goes to her pocket, where the chalk is. It stays there.]
SERA: A hand. Last time it was two fingers.
WREN: Is a hand more than two fingers?
SERA: It's five.
WREN: Five! I did five in one night! Is that a lot? It feels like a lot!
WREN: Can I see? Is there a looking glass? Is the window a looking glass if it's dark enough?
SERA: It's light out. You'll see the harbor.
WREN: Then I'll look in the harbor! It's a big looking glass. It's the biggest one!
[She presses her face to the glass. Far below, the slips, the boats sitting on them, the breakwater.]
WREN: Sera, the boats are all in. Every one. Is it a holiday?
SERA: It's not a holiday.
WREN: Then why's nobody out? They're always out. Bram says boats are like cats, they hate being in.
SERA: Ask Bram.
WREN: You know. Your face knows.
SERA: Nobody's going out on a tide they can't read.
[WREN turns from the glass and looks at her properly.]
WREN: Is that your tables? Are your tables the ones they can't read?
SERA: Yes.
WREN: Can you write them again? Bigger? I could help. I can reach the top of the wall now.
SERA: It's not the size that's wrong.
SERA: It moved at two. You were asleep under that window at two.
WREN: Did I grow in my sleep? Can you do that? Is that allowed?
SERA: I don't know what's allowed. I know when.
WREN: When's good! When's half of it! Bram says when's the half of a supper that matters.
[Footsteps on the stair, slow and heavy. Two sets.]
[BRAM comes up the last turn with a loaf under his arm. His oilskin is still wet. The Keeper comes up behind him.]
BRAM: Bread, still warm, and a keeper, still damp. Nell's in. Patience is in. Everybody's—
[He sees Wren. He stops on the top step.]
WREN: Bram! Bram, look! I'm bigger than the lantern now!
[She stands beside his storm lantern where it hangs by the door. It used to hang at her eyes. It hangs at her chest.]
[BRAM says nothing for a moment.]
BRAM: So you are.
BRAM: Then you'd better stay where I—
[He doesn't finish. He puts the loaf down on the cradle step, carefully, as if it might break.]
WREN: Where you what? Where you can find me? I'm easy to find now! I'm the long one!
BRAM: Come here. Sleeves.
[He kneels. He unrolls the left sleeve. Three turns come out. He rolls it once. Her wrist is out. He stops.]
[He does the right. Once. He stops.]
WREN: One! Only one! Is one better than three? It's fewer. Fewer's lighter.
BRAM: One's... tidy. One's a very tidy number.
SERA: It moved at two, Bram.
BRAM: Sera.
SERA: I'm saying what's there.
BRAM: I know what's there. I've had my eyes open all night. They've seen plenty.
[He glances at the Keeper. Then away, quickly, to the bread.]
KEEPER: Bram. Sit down.
BRAM: I'm fine sat up. Bread. Tear it. Everybody. Before Sera writes on it.
WREN: Bram, your coat's all wet. Were you in the sea? Did you go in the sea without me?
BRAM: Near enough, love. The sea came up to say hello. Rude of it, at that hour.
WREN: What hour?
BRAM: The hour nobody's meant to be up but bakers and trouble. Never mind it.
BRAM: So. Funny thing about last night. Everybody's home. That's the whole story. It's a short one.
WREN: Short stories are the best ones. You can have two.
SERA: My tables were two hours and ten minutes wrong.
SERA: I can correct nine minutes. I can correct nineteen. I can't correct two hours.
BRAM: Then don't. Not today. Today eat bread.
SERA: I'll not print more. Not till I know what to print.
[BRAM tears the loaf in four. He gives the Keeper a piece without looking at them.]
WREN: Bram. Can I go and show Tam? He said the coat would want taking in. Now it won't! Not much!
BRAM: Show him. Go on. Keeper, go with her.
[He says it to the bread.]
KEEPER: You should sleep, Bram.
BRAM: I'll sleep when the bread's gone. Go on. She's quicker than you now. She's quicker than me.
WREN: I'm going to take the stairs two at a time. Can I? Is that allowed?
BRAM: Count them for me. Out loud. So I know where you are.
WREN: I will!
[She goes. At the top of the stair she stops, and looks at the underside of the rail a moment too long. Then she's off.]
[Her feet on the stair: quick, then quicker, two steps at a time. No counting comes up.]
[BRAM listens for it. It doesn't come.]
SERA: She's not counting.
BRAM: She's forgotten. That's all. She's excited.
[He picks up a piece of bread. He doesn't eat it.]
[Skip summary: On the morning after the early tide, Wren wakes a hand taller. Sera says the Belt moved at two, while Wren slept. Bram, back from a long night at the harbor, rolls her sleeves only once and goes quiet; he can't finish his sentence about keeping her where he can see her, and doesn't look the keeper in the eye. Wren runs down the stair two at a time to show Tam, and forgets to count.]

### Scene 2 — The quay, the loft stair
[The harbor, mid-morning. Boats sit on the slips; nobody has gone out. Women gut herring at the long table by the third slip. A wooden stair climbs the smokehouse wall to the net-loft door, twenty steps, worn grey.]
[WREN comes down the quay at a run, the Keeper behind her. She jumps a bollard. Then the next one. She lands both.]
[Objective: Show Tam.]
WREN: Good morning! Good morning! Morning, gulls!
WREN: Keeper, I can jump the bollards! I couldn't jump the bollards yesterday! I had to go round!
KEEPER: You'll go in the harbor.
WREN: I won't! My legs know where they end now!
[Two children on an upturned boat stop their skipping rope. They stare.]
CHILD: Who's that?
CHILD: That's Bram Hollis's girl.
CHILD: She's never. His girl's little.
WREN: I was little! I'm the same one! I'm just more of it!
[At the gutting table, a woman with a baby on her hip and two more children at her skirts stops with her knife in a herring. She watches Wren all the way along the quay.]
[WREN stops at the table, bright as a new penny.]
WREN: Good morning! Can I help? I can reach the far end of the table now. Look. I can reach the bucket.
[The woman doesn't answer. She looks at Wren's face, then at the cuffs, then at the face again.]
WREN: I'm Wren. From the tower. I was here last week. I was the little one. Remember?
[The woman puts her knife down. She picks up the baby, though the baby was quiet.]
WREN: Is the baby asleep? Should I be quieter? I can be quieter. I've got a quiet voice now. It's lower.
KEEPER: Wren. Tam's waiting.
WREN: Bye! Bye, baby! Bye, fish!
[The woman watches her go. She doesn't pick the knife up again.]
[At the smokehouse door, HESTER wipes her hands on her apron, twice.]
HESTER: Who's this long article?
WREN: Hester! It's me! I'm on your slate!
HESTER: You were on my slate at four foot nothing. What've you been eating?
WREN: Pie! Bram says pie does it!
HESTER: Then you're on my slate twice.
[HESTER looks at her face, hard, the way she looks at a fish. Then at the Keeper.]
HESTER: Keeper. You and me want a word. Not now. When she's not stood there shining at me.
WREN: I'm not shining! Am I shining?
HESTER: You're grinning, sprat. It's near enough.
[From the top of the loft stair comes a mumble.]
TAM: Mm. Mmph. Hang on.
[TAM sits on the top step with Nell's net across his knees. He takes the needle out of his teeth.]
TAM: Wren?
WREN: Tam! Look! Look at me! Don't look at the net, look at me!
[She takes the stair at a run, two at a time, and stops on the step below him. She stands on tiptoe and puts her palm flat on the top of the loft door frame.]
WREN: I can touch the top! Tam, I can touch the top of the door!
TAM: Well. That's a new one.
[He looks at her: the coat, the hem, the one roll at each cuff.]
TAM: You've come on. That's a hand, that is. Near enough.
WREN: Sera says a hand is five!
TAM: Sera would. I'd say it's a hand.
WREN: I'm up to your shoulder! Stand up. Stand up and see!
[TAM stands. The top of her head is at his shoulder.]
TAM: Shoulder. Last week you were my belt.
WREN: I'm going to be your ears next! Then your hat!
TAM: I've not got a hat. It's at the bottom of the harbor.
WREN: Then I'll be where your hat would be!
TAM: Mind you don't go in the harbor after it.
WREN: Tam, I can see over everything from here. Over the boats. Over the smokehouse chimney nearly.
WREN: Is this what it's like for Bram all the time? No wonder he's cheerful. Everything's so small and tidy.
TAM: Bram's cheerful because he eats. You can see the top of his head from the loft roof. It's not tidy.
WREN: I'm going to see the top of everybody's head. I'm going to tell them what's up there.
TAM: What's up there?
WREN: Hair, mostly. And Pell's got a gull feather. Did you know? It's stuck in his hat.
TAM: That's been there since summer. He thinks it's lucky.
[TAM looks at her sleeves. He can't help himself.]
TAM: Your sleeves are nearly right.
WREN: Nobody touches the sleeves. You know that.
TAM: I wasn't going to touch them. I was going to say they're nearly right.
TAM: Nearly's the best bit. After that it's just right, and right's boring.
WREN: Is it? Is right boring?
TAM: Ask Nell. She's always right. She's never once enjoyed it.
[WREN sits down on the step beside him. Her knees come up nearly to her chin.]
WREN: Tam. Teach me to mend. My hands reach now. They reach everything.
TAM: It's not reaching. It's knots. Knots is patience.
WREN: I've got patience! I've got loads! I waited all night to be big!
TAM: You were asleep.
WREN: Patiently!
[TAM considers this seriously.]
TAM: Nell's net first. She wants it in three days, so it's two.
TAM: Day after tomorrow. Dusk. Here, on this stair.
WREN: Why the stair? Why dusk?
TAM: Best light in the harbor's on this stair at dusk. Sun comes straight down the lane.
TAM: Hester won't light a lamp till it's dark. Oil's money. Light's free on the stair.
WREN: Day after tomorrow. Dusk. The stair. I'll remember. Keeper, remember for me as well.
KEEPER: I'll remember.
WREN: Good. Now there's two of us remembering. That's what Sera does.
[Down on the quay, the woman at the gutting table is still watching. So are the children. So is a man on the next slip, with a coil of rope he has stopped coiling.]
WREN: Tam? Why's everybody looking? Is it because I'm long?
TAM: It's because you're new.
TAM: Saltreach doesn't get new. It got you, and the keeper, and Jory back. That's ten years of new.
WREN: Is new bad?
TAM: Couldn't say. I've never been it.
WREN: Jory's new. Is he bad?
TAM: Jory's old. He just went away a while. That's different. He'll wear back in.
WREN: Like a step?
TAM: Like a step. Ninth one, probably. Grumpy, but you'd miss it.
[Skip summary: Wren races down the quay, jumping bollards she used to walk round, and the harbor stops to stare; children don't believe she's Bram's girl, and Hester calls her a long article and wants a word with the keeper later. On the net-loft stair Wren shows Tam she can touch the top of the door frame. Tam promises to teach her a mending knot at dusk on the stair, the day after tomorrow.]

### Scene 3 — The crater road, dusk
[The marsh road north at dusk. Reeds on both sides, leaning. BRAM walks with the storm lantern lit. WREN walks beside him. The Keeper walks a step behind.]
[Behind them, high on the headland, a small light climbs the dark tower: Sera, going up to count.]
[Objective: Walk the crater road.]
WREN: Bram. Bram, look at my feet.
BRAM: I'm looking at the road, love. Road's the job.
WREN: I'm walking your walk! I don't have to run to keep up! I've had to run since the crater!
BRAM: So you have.
WREN: Left, right, left. Your left and my left are the same left now.
BRAM: They always were. You just had more of them.
[BRAM lifts the lantern and looks ahead at the reeds. He doesn't look back at the Keeper.]
BRAM: Sera'll be saying twenty-eight about now. Twice. Once when she sees it, once when it's written.
KEEPER: Bram, about last night—
BRAM: So. Funny thing about this road.
BRAM: Every dusk it's the same road. Same ruts. Same reeds. Same nothing.
BRAM: And every dusk I walk it like it's going to be different. That's patrols. That's the whole art.
[At the pan-house end of the road, a woman stands on the step with a lamp and a pencil behind her ear.]
MAGS: Bram Hollis. Keeper. Under "road". And— no. Hold still. Who's this?
WREN: It's me! Wren!
MAGS: Wren was the little one. I've had her down as the little one all week.
WREN: I'm the long one now! You'll have to rub it out!
MAGS: I don't rub out. I cross through. Long one. There.
WREN: Can I see? Is my name in the book?
MAGS: You're under "Bram's lot". Bram, Keeper, little one. Now it's Bram, Keeper, long one.
WREN: Is "long one" a name?
MAGS: In my book it is. Ferris is "hat". Abel's "Abel". You'll do.
[MAGS taps the pencil on her pay-tin. She looks at Wren a moment longer than she needs to.]
MAGS: My feet don't like it, Bram.
BRAM: Your feet don't like anything, Mags.
MAGS: They liked her small. Go on. Bowl's been sparkling since four.
[Out on the road ahead, two low shapes of greenish glass come over the rise, heads down, walking at a fixed pace. They make no sound.]
BRAM: There they are. Evening, dogs.
WREN: Glass dogs! Bram, can I—
BRAM: Behind the lamp.
WREN: I'm taller than the lamp!
BRAM: Then behind it, taller. Go on.
[Battle — Stage 2-2-2: Crater Patrol. Enemies: Glass Hounds x2, then x3. Win condition: keep the road. Wren starts the fight brighter than in Act I; her growth stacks faster.]
[BRAM plants the lantern. The first hound walks into the edge of its light and stops, then turns to walk round it.]
[Every hound's faceless head turns toward Wren at once. She is glowing already.]
WREN: They're looking! Hello! I'm the same one! I'm just more of it!
BRAM: Don't chat to them!
WREN: They chatted first! With their heads!
BRAM: Heads isn't chatting. Keeper, left one. Wren, stay in the light.
WREN: I'm in it! Half of me's in it. The top half's out. Is the top half allowed?
BRAM: The top half's the half I'm worried about!
[WREN steps out to the edge of the light, sure-footed, and strikes. A hound breaks across the legs and falls into warm sand.]
WREN: Did you see? Keeper, did you see? I reached it! I didn't have to jump!
[A second hound comes in low, straight at her, and stops a pace off. Its head lowers until its blank face is level with her knee. It holds there, tilted.]
WREN: It came to my knee this time. On the bridge one came to my toes.
WREN: Is it smaller, or am I?
[The Keeper strikes it sideways. It falls to sand. WREN looks at the heap. She doesn't look sorry, exactly.]
[Three more come over the rise. WREN's light swells. When the Starfall comes down across the road, it is wider than it was at the bend, and brighter. The reeds on both sides flatten in it.]
[When it's gone, the road is sand from rut to rut.]
WREN: Did I do good? I did good!
WREN: I didn't even need a boot to stand behind! Bram!
[BRAM is standing very still with the lantern raised. His face, in its light, has no joke in it at all.]
BRAM: You did good, love. Come here a minute.
[She comes. He takes her by the cuff, the one rolled cuff, and holds on to it.]
WREN: Is something wrong? Did I do it too big?
BRAM: No. No, you did it the right size.
BRAM: It's the road. Road's quiet now. I like it quiet.
WREN: You said every dusk it's the same nothing. That wasn't nothing! That was the best something!
WREN: Did you see the big one go down? I did it with my whole arm!
BRAM: I saw. Whole road saw. Mags's feet saw.
BRAM: That's the trouble with somethings. Home. Supper. Sera's had twenty-eight on her own long enough.
[He turns back toward the harbor. He doesn't wait for the Keeper. He doesn't look round.]
[Skip summary: On the evening Crater Patrol, Wren keeps pace with Bram for the first time, and Mags doesn't recognize her. Glass Hounds come down the road and turn their heads to her. One stops at her knee, where at the bridge it stopped at her toes. Her Starfall is wider and brighter than before. She is delighted, and Bram is frightened, and still doesn't look at the keeper.]

### Scene 4 — The marsh road, home
[The marsh road south, full dark. The lights of Saltreach ahead. BRAM walks with the lantern; WREN beside him, matching his stride on purpose; the Keeper a step behind.]
[Objective: Walk home.]
WREN: Bram. When you were as big as me now, what did you do?
BRAM: As big as you now? Fourteen, I'd say. Fifteen.
BRAM: I carried my grandad's supper up the tower stair, and oil after it, and I knocked the ninth step every time.
WREN: Did you light the big lamp?
BRAM: First time he let me was the night I turned fifteen. He stood behind me with his hand on my back.
BRAM: I was so careful I took a quarter of an hour over the wick. Boats were queuing at the bar, he said. He was lying.
WREN: Was it bright?
BRAM: Brightest thing I ever did. Brighter than I've been since.
WREN: Brighter than me?
[BRAM doesn't answer straight away.]
BRAM: Different bright. Mine stayed in the lamp.
WREN: Mine stays in me. Mostly. It goes out the ends a bit when there's dogs.
BRAM: I noticed the ends.
WREN: Did your grandad see you light it? From the bottom?
BRAM: He went down to the slip after, to look up at it. Said he'd never once seen it from outside.
BRAM: Forty years up the top of it. Never once stood and looked at it lit.
WREN: Did he like it?
BRAM: He said it was smaller than he'd thought. Then he said it was the right size. Then he went to bed.
WREN: I want to see it from outside. The big one. Lit.
BRAM: So do I, love. So do I.
[They walk. The reeds hiss either side.]
WREN: Bram. Why don't you look at the Keeper?
[BRAM stops walking for half a step. Then he keeps going.]
BRAM: I look at the Keeper.
WREN: You look at the Keeper's boots. And the bread. And the lantern. All day.
BRAM: So. Funny thing about looking.
BRAM: When you've been up all night, love, your eyes go where they like. Mine like boots.
WREN: Is that true? It sounds like a pie thing.
BRAM: A pie thing?
WREN: Like "pie makes you big." Ivo said "Does he" when you said that. He said it in his flat voice.
BRAM: Ivo says everything in his flat voice. He's only got the one.
[The Keeper says nothing. BRAM glances back at them, quickly, and away to the lights.]
WREN: Keeper. Are you cross with Bram?
[Choice a: "No."]
[Choice b: "Ask Bram."]
[Choices converge.]
WREN: Then nobody's cross. Good. I don't like cross. It makes the road longer.
BRAM: Everybody's tired, that's all. Tired looks like cross from the outside. Ask Hester.
BRAM: Tonight, Wren, when we go up. Count the stair for me.
WREN: I forgot this morning. Was that bad?
BRAM: It wasn't bad. I just like knowing where you are. Out loud. Every step.
WREN: Even when I'm big?
BRAM: Especially when you're big. Big's further up. I can't see as far up as I used to.
WREN: Then I'll count very loud. They'll hear me on the breakwater.
BRAM: Good. Let them.
WREN: Keeper, if you hear me from the breakwater, wave the lamp. Two dips. Like Bram does.
BRAM: The keeper's got a lamp to mind, love.
WREN: Two dips is minding. It's minding twice.
[Skip summary: Walking home from the crater road, Bram tells Wren he lit the old lamp once, at about her size now, with his grandad's hand on his back. Wren asks why he won't look at the keeper; Bram blames a night without sleep and changes the subject. He asks her to count the stair out loud tonight, so he knows where she is.]

### Scene 5 — The smokehouse, noon
[Day 19. Hester's smokehouse at noon: every bench full of fishermen who haven't gone out. Nell sits at the end of one with her pipe filled and unlit. At the next table, a woman with a baby on her hip eats standing up.]
[HESTER comes down the benches with a pot and the Keeper's bowl.]
[Objective: Get fed.]
HESTER: Sit. Herring, bread. You've the face of a man who walked a road twice.
KEEPER: Once. With Bram and Wren.
HESTER: Then Bram walked it twice and you're wearing it. Eat.
[She sits down across from the Keeper, which she never does. She wipes her hands on her apron, twice.]
HESTER: That word I wanted. Out with it. What's she eating up that tower that I'm not feeding her?
KEEPER: Bread. Your pie.
HESTER: Then it's not food.
[A pause. HESTER doesn't write anything down.]
HESTER: Yesterday morning she came down my quay a hand longer than she went up it.
HESTER: By noon I'd had nine in here asking who the long girl was.
HESTER: Nine. Not one of them hungry. That's how I knew they meant it.
KEEPER: What did you tell them?
HESTER: Bram Hollis's girl, on my slate. One soup. Same as ever.
HESTER: Ada Marr asked if Bram had taken in a lodger.
HESTER: Pell asked if she was the same one. Old Mrs Cole asked if it was catching.
KEEPER: Is that bad?
HESTER: It's faces, Keeper. I don't listen to what folk say in here. Everybody lies in a smokehouse.
HESTER: I watch what their faces do when they say it.
HESTER: Some of them smiled. Good. Some of them didn't. Those are the ones I watch.
HESTER: And Bram Hollis went past my door yesterday at seven and didn't stop for bread.
HESTER: Twenty years he's never once not stopped. He bought it at the baker's. The baker's.
KEEPER: He was tired.
HESTER: Tired's a face. I know tired. That was another one.
HESTER: Who's asking? Nobody. I'm only saying. I don't write faces down.
[She looks at the Keeper's face now, the same way. The Keeper looks at the herring.]
HESTER: Hm. Eat your fish.
[At the end of the bench, NELL slaps a flat hand on the table.]
NELL: Pike. More bread, if it's going on the slate anyway.
HESTER: Everything's going on the slate, Garrow. Your whole crew's been on my benches since the bar.
NELL: Nobody's going out on a tide nobody can read. That's not sentiment. That's sense.
NELL: Surveyor's tables were two hours wrong. Two hours. On a flat sea.
HESTER: She'll fix them.
NELL: She said she won't print more. I heard it at the slip post. She's not a liar, that one.
NELL: That's the worst thing about her.
[NELL looks out of the door, up at the white noon sky.]
NELL: Three times now, it's gone. Before this autumn, never once in my life.
NELL: First time, weeks. Then days. Then in the night, while decent folk were asleep.
HESTER: Not everybody was asleep.
[At the next table the woman with the baby turns round.]
ADA: I wasn't.
ADA: Up at two with this one. Teething. I was at the window walking her.
ADA: There was a hole in the cloud. And it went. Right across the hole, like a bead on a wire.
HESTER: Ada Marr. Sit down and eat it, you'll get indigestion.
ADA: I'll eat standing. I'm not stopping.
ADA: I'll tell you what I told mine this morning. All three of them.
ADA: Shutters to at dark. Bairns in. Nobody at the window.
ADA: Don't look up after supper. That's my house.
NELL: That's a sailor's rule turned inside out. We look up after supper. It's how we get home.
ADA: Then you look, Nell Garrow. You've got a boat. I've got three bairns and a window.
[The door bangs open. WREN ducks under the lintel, which she never used to have to do, and comes in laughing.]
WREN: Hester! I had to duck! Did you see? I ducked your door!
HESTER: I saw. Mind my beam, I've only got the one.
WREN: Is my pot there? With my name? W, R, E, N?
HESTER: It's there. It's the little one.
[She looks Wren up and down.]
HESTER: I'll need a bigger pot.
WREN: Can it be the biggest? Can it be Bram's size?
HESTER: It'll be the size you are by the week's end. Sit. Eat. Not by Ada, she's going.
[ADA has already picked up her basket. She goes out wide round Wren, baby turned away, and doesn't look back.]
WREN: Bye, Ada! Bye, baby!
[ADA doesn't answer. WREN sits down beside the Keeper, still smiling, and takes her pot in both hands.]
WREN: Keeper, the pot's tiny. Was I ever this tiny? Was it only last week?
HESTER: Last week, sprat. It's in the book.
[HESTER looks at Wren's face as she eats. She looks for a long time.]
[Skip summary: At the smokehouse on day 19, Hester tells the keeper the harbor has been coming in to ask who the long girl is, and that she watches their faces, not their words. Nell's crew won't go out on tides nobody can read, and she says the sky has moved three times now, faster each time. Ada Marr, who saw the night shift through a hole in the cloud, says her house rule is now "Don't look up after supper." Wren ducks under the door, and Hester says she'll need a bigger pot.]

### Scene 6 — The slip
[The third slip, afternoon. CORRA sits on a bollard with her apron open, shards of crater glass laid out on a cloth. Nobody is buying.]
[WREN and the Keeper come along the quay. CORRA sees Wren coming, and stands up.]
[Objective: Find Corra.]
CORRA: Keep. And— oh, you are joking.
WREN: Corra! I've come to buy luck! Have you got any long luck?
CORRA: You're taller than me.
WREN: Am I? Stand still!
CORRA: You weren't taller than me last week. You came up to my chin. I remember, you breathed on my apron.
WREN: Back to back! Bram does it with the door! Keeper, look and say!
[They stand back to back on the slip. WREN is half a head over.]
KEEPER: Wren.
CORRA: Fine. Fine! It's the coat. The coat's got a heel.
WREN: Coats don't have heels!
CORRA: That one might. It's Bram's. Bram's things are all bigger than they look.
WREN: That's true! His spoon is. His spoon is a shovel.
CORRA: That's a loaf and a half of difference. That's what that is. A loaf and a half.
[CORRA sits back down on the bollard, hard.]
CORRA: Does it hurt? Growing?
WREN: It itches. Then it stops. Then you can reach things.
CORRA: My brother cried for a week when he grew. Said his knees were coming off.
WREN: My knees are on. Look. Both of them.
CORRA: I can see they're on.
[She rattles the glass in her apron, then stops, as if she doesn't like the sound.]
CORRA: Sera counted me at the crater. On her hand. Before you.
WREN: She counted me first. On her hand. I was on her hand before you were on her anything.
CORRA: She counted me second. That's still counting.
WREN: Is it? Is second still counting?
CORRA: It's still a number. Sera says a number's a number.
WREN: Then we're both on her hand. That's nice. We're neighbours.
[CORRA opens her mouth to argue, and finds she doesn't want to.]
CORRA: You're not supposed to be nice about it. It ruins it.
[WREN looks out past the breakwater at the water, which is higher on the slip than the tide table nailed beside it says.]
WREN: Everything's leaning a little. Have you noticed?
CORRA: Leaning?
WREN: The reeds. The tide. Abel's pony, when it's stood still.
WREN: Everything's leaning a little bit east. Like listening.
CORRA: Tide doesn't listen.
WREN: Doesn't it? It feels like it does. Where did I get that? I don't know where I got that.
[She laughs.]
WREN: Maybe I'm leaning too. Is that why I'm long? I'm just leaning upward?
[A woman comes down the quay with a basket and three children: ADA MARR. She sees Wren. She stops. Then she steers the children out round the end of the slip, the long way, on the far side of the bollards.]
CHILD: Mam, why are we going round?
ADA: Because I said.
CHILD: But it's longer round.
ADA: Then it's longer. Walk.
[WREN watches them go the long way. Her smile stays where it is. It's a moment before she speaks.]
WREN: That's the lady from the fish table. With the baby. I said good morning to the baby.
CORRA: Ada Marr. She's got three. She guts for Hester.
WREN: Corra. Why did they go round?
CORRA: ...She's always in a hurry, Ada. Baby's teething.
WREN: But going round is slower.
CORRA: Fine. I don't know. Do you want to buy luck or not? It's a penny.
WREN: I haven't got a penny. I've got a slate.
CORRA: Luck's not on Hester's slate. Here.
[She holds out a small shard. WREN takes it. In her palm, it warms. Both of them see it warm.]
[CORRA takes her hand back quickly.]
CORRA: Keep it. On the house. Don't tell Mags.
WREN: Is it lucky?
CORRA: It's a penny's worth of lucky. That's what it says on the cloth.
WREN: Does lucky go warm? Is that how you know?
CORRA: Mine don't. Not for me. Not like that.
[She folds the cloth over the rest of her glass, quickly, and ties it.]
CORRA: I'm going. Mags'll want me. Tell Sera I said— no. Don't tell her anything.
[Skip summary: On the slip, Corra finds Wren is half a head taller than her and blames the coat. They argue about who Sera counted first and end up "neighbours" on her hand. Wren says everything is leaning a little east, and can't say where she got it. Ada Marr walks her children the long way round to avoid Wren. Corra gives her a shard, and they both see it warm in Wren's hand.]

### Scene 7 — The lamp room, dusk
[The lamp room at dusk. SERA kneels at the bottom course with the sextant. The Keeper holds the chalk.]
[Objective: Hold the chalk.]
SERA: Twenty-eight. Same as last night. Same as two in the morning.
SERA: Small. Next to the last one.
[The Keeper marks it. SERA rubs the edge with her thumb until it's smaller.]
SERA: Smaller.
KEEPER: How much is left?
SERA: Less than half the course.
KEEPER: And at this size?
SERA: At this size, a week's gone in a hand's width. Before, a hand's width lasted a month.
SERA: It's not the size that's changed.
[Feet on the stair, quick, two at a time. Then a heavier pair behind, slower, carrying a pot.]
WREN: Sera! I'm up! I came up in jumps!
BRAM: How many, love?
WREN: I forgot! I was thinking about knots!
BRAM: You counted last night. All the way up. Loud as a bell.
WREN: I did! I'll do it again tomorrow. Tonight my head was full.
BRAM: Knots.
WREN: Tam's going to teach me. Tomorrow at dusk, on his stair. Proper ones, for Nell's net.
BRAM: Is he.
[BRAM sets the pot on the cradle step. He sets out bowls: one, two, three, four, and the spare. He hands the Keeper theirs without looking up.]
BRAM: Fish and potato. Hester's fish, my potato, stair dust. Eat while it's hot. Sera, that means you.
SERA: After the count.
BRAM: The count's twenty-eight. I heard you on the stair. So did the ninth step.
[WREN goes to SERA at the wall. She stands up very straight.]
WREN: Sera. Am I the same as this morning? Yesterday morning, I mean. Am I the same?
SERA: Stand still.
[SERA looks. Her hand goes to her pocket, and stays there.]
SERA: Same.
WREN: Same! Good. I want to be same for a bit. Is that a funny thing to want?
SERA: No. Same is exact.
WREN: Exact is better than a lot. You said.
WREN: Sera. I can reach the top of the wall now. I could hold the chalk. For the high ones.
SERA: There aren't any high ones left. Only the bottom course.
WREN: Then I could hold it low. I can bend. Look, I bend.
SERA: The keeper holds the chalk.
WREN: When the keeper's not here, then?
SERA: The keeper's always here.
[WREN looks at the Keeper, and grins, and doesn't argue.]
[She goes to the glass and looks out over the dark headland, at the three stars sitting over it.]
WREN: There's ever so much room east of it.
[SERA turns. The sextant comes up, east of the Belt, into the dark.]
SERA: Where?
WREN: Just there. Past it. Room. Lots of it.
SERA: What do you see?
WREN: Nothing. Dark. Lots of dark.
WREN: Is dark a thing you can have lots of? Or is it just less of something else?
[SERA holds the sextant on the empty dark a long moment. She lowers it. She writes nothing.]
SERA: There's nothing there.
WREN: No. That's what I said. Room.
[WREN laughs at herself and sits down by the pot.]
WREN: I don't know where that came from. Is it the pie talking?
BRAM: It's always the pie talking. Eat some more, see what else it says.
[SERA sits at last, and takes her bowl. BRAM watches her eat, and doesn't make a joke about it. He is quiet.]
SERA: You're quiet.
BRAM: I'm chewing.
SERA: You chew loud, as a rule.
BRAM: I'm chewing considerate tonight. It's a new thing I'm trying.
[SERA opens her tide book to the back page, writes something short, and closes it.]
BRAM: Abel came to the slip today. Wants to walk the road with me tomorrow.
BRAM: Says the reeds want watching. Says they're lying further every dusk.
SERA: They are. He's right.
BRAM: So I'll walk it with Abel. Tomorrow. And his pony, I expect, she goes where he goes.
KEEPER: I'll come.
BRAM: No.
[A beat. BRAM looks at the bowl in his hands.]
BRAM: You stay with her. She's on Tam's stair at dusk. Somebody ought to be at the bottom of it.
BRAM: That's not nothing. That's a job. That's the job I'd pick, if I wasn't picking the road.
KEEPER: Then pick it.
BRAM: Abel asked. You don't turn Abel down. He takes it out on the pony.
[He still doesn't look at the Keeper. SERA looks from one of them to the other.]
SERA: What's wrong with you two?
BRAM: Nothing's wrong. I'm chewing. They're keeping. Everyone's very busy. Wren, love. Seconds?
WREN: Yes! Can I have the bit with the skin? The skin's the best bit, Tam says. Tam says nearly's the best bit, too.
WREN: Is everything the best bit, if you ask Tam?
BRAM: Most things. He's a mender. They're like that.
[Skip summary: At dusk on day 19 Sera chalks twenty-eight, writing smaller; the last course is going fast. Wren asks to stay "the same for a bit". At the glass she says there's "ever so much room" east of the Belt; Sera looks, and writes nothing. Bram is quiet, and says he'll walk the crater road tomorrow with Abel and leave the keeper to mind Wren on Tam's stair. Sera asks what's wrong with the two of them, and Bram changes the subject.]

### Scene 8 — The net loft
[Day 20, afternoon. Inside the net loft: nets on every beam, the smell of tar and smoke, the HOLLIS crate under a blanket by the chimney. TAM works on Nell's net. JORY sits in the window with his collar up, looking at the breakwater.]
[WREN sits on the crate, scratching her arm through the coat.]
[Objective: Finish Nell's net.]
TAM: Sit still.
WREN: I am sitting still. It's my arm that isn't.
TAM: Then sit your arm still.
WREN: It itches! It's the inside of my arm. Can you scratch the inside of an arm?
TAM: Couldn't say. I've never had one off.
JORY: She's been at it since noon. Like a dog with fleas.
WREN: I haven't got fleas! Have I got fleas? Keeper, look in my hair!
KEEPER: No fleas.
JORY: Not my business.
[He goes back to looking at the breakwater. Then he glances once at the Keeper, as if checking the lamp is lit, and back.]
WREN: Jory, why did you come back? Everybody says you won't say.
JORY: Packet was going this way.
WREN: That's not a reason. That's a boat.
JORY: Boats are reasons. Ask Nell.
WREN: Tam came back from the end of the breakwater once, Bram says. Green.
TAM: Wind took my hat.
JORY: He cried.
TAM: I didn't cry. I was wet in the face. It was a wet day.
[WREN laughs, then scratches harder.]
WREN: Tam, is it nearly dusk? Is it nearly the knot?
TAM: Hour yet. I've the last corner. Nell's coming for it at dark.
WREN: Can I watch the corner?
TAM: You can hold the edge. Hold it flat. Don't help.
[WREN holds the edge of the net. TAM's needle goes in and out, fast, without him looking.]
WREN: How do you know where it goes without looking?
TAM: Hands know. You tell them once, and then they know, and you can look at something else.
WREN: Like stairs.
TAM: Like stairs. Except a stair's always in the same place.
WREN: Not ours. Ours changes its mind.
[She pulls a loop the wrong way. It snarls.]
TAM: That's not a knot. That's a decision.
WREN: Is it a bad decision?
TAM: It's a firm one. We'll take it out.
WREN: Can't we keep it? It tried very hard.
TAM: A net's not for trying. It's for holding. Fish don't care how hard you tried.
WREN: Fish are very harsh.
TAM: Fish are the harshest people I know. Except Nell.
[Down in the lane, a lantern goes by, lit in daylight. BRAM's voice comes up through the window.]
BRAM: Keeper! You up there?
[The Keeper goes to the window. BRAM stands in the lane with the storm lantern and a pie under his arm. Behind him, ABEL waits with his reed-hook and his pony.]

### Scene 10 — The smokehouse
[The smokehouse, the same hour. TAM sits on the end of a bench with both hands in the Keeper's bucket. HESTER kneels in front of him with a pot of honey and a roll of linen. The fires are banked.]
[The door is full of people. So is the window. Half the harbor has followed them in and stopped just inside, the way you'd stop at the edge of a lamp.]

### Scene 13 — The net loft, night
[The net loft by the light of one candle. TAM sits on the HOLLIS crate with both hands wrapped in linen to the wrist, held up in front of him like a dog begging. JORY sits beside him with a bowl, spooning soup into him. HESTER stands over both.]
