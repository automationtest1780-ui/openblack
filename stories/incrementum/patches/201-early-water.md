## Planning sheet (gacha 14.2)

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**

He is stuck in the tower with a sleeping child while the boat he grew up beside hits the bar. Cost: at three in the morning he cuts the night's reading out of the breakwater log with his own knife, asks the keeper to say nothing, and carries it in his oilskin. ## Script

### Scene 1 — The slip, morning
[The harbor at mid-morning, two days after supper in the lamp room. Sera's tide table is nailed to the slip post. Under it runs a column of her chalk corrections: 9, 12, 19.]
[Sera stands at the foot of the slip with the water round her boots, a watch in one hand and chalk in the other. Old Pell sits on his upturned crate. The Keeper comes along the quay from the breakwater with the logbook under one arm.]
[Objective: Read the morning water.]
SERA: Keeper. Slip. Morning water. You're late, it turned without you.
KEEPER: When?
SERA: Twenty-one minutes to ten. The table says ten past.
SERA: Thirty-one minutes.
[She chalks 31 on the post, under the 19.]
PELL: Thirty-one. Half an hour, near enough.
SERA: Thirty-one isn't near anything. It's thirty-one.
PELL: Half an hour's what a man can hold in his head, Surveyor. Thirty-one's for you.
PELL: Keeper. You've been past the customs house door this morning?
KEEPER: "Weather."
PELL: "Weather. The council is watching it." In black ink, not red.
PELL: First time in my life the council's watched anything. I went and watched it watching.
PELL: Door didn't move. I'll give it that.
SERA: One word. It used to be two.
PELL: Fog-madness is off it, at least. Gage scrubbed it himself. Six o'clock, in his good coat.
PELL: Said his grandad would want it scrubbed by somebody who meant it.
SERA: Keeper. Step down. You're reading it from the dry.
KEEPER: I'm reading it fine from here.
SERA: You're reading it from where it was supposed to be. Stand where it is.
[The Keeper steps down. The water comes over their boots, past the line of weed the table says it should reach at ten.]
SERA: There. Ten o'clock's water, at twenty to.
PELL: She's done that every morning since the shift. Stood in it to the ankles.
PELL: Yesterday the shins. I'm only saying. I'm not saying anything.
[Bram comes down the slip with a coil of line over one shoulder. He stops at the edge of the wet.]
BRAM: Morning, Sera. You're standing in the sea.
SERA: The sea's standing where I am. It got here first.
BRAM: Funny thing about the sea. It doesn't care who got there first. It just keeps coming.
BRAM: Come up a step. Your feet are blue. I can see them through the boots.
SERA: Not till I've got the turn.
BRAM: You've got the turn. You wrote it on the post. I saw the three and the one.
[He holds out a hand. She looks at the water, then at the hand. She takes it and comes up one step. Exactly one.]
BRAM: One step. I'll take one step. I'm a reasonable man.
SERA: Keeper. The breakwater has a gauge. The painted post inside the end.
KEEPER: The stick with the marks on.
SERA: Read it at three. Every night. For the council book.
SERA: Then find the hour on my table where the water should stand that high.
SERA: The difference is the number. Write it in minutes.
KEEPER: Early or late?
SERA: Early. It's only ever been early.
SERA: Two books, not one. Mine at the slip. Yours at the breakwater.
SERA: If they agree, it's true. If they don't, one of us is standing in the wrong place.
PELL: It'll be the sea standing in the wrong place, Surveyor. It's been at it all month.
BRAM: Thirty-one, then. What's thirty-one in plain?
SERA: Thirty-one is plain.
BRAM: In supper, then. How long's thirty-one in supper?
SERA: Two bowls. If you don't talk.
BRAM: I always talk. Call it one bowl and a bit of bread.
[Pell looks out past the breakwater at a long dark shape on the slip: Patience, her new planks pale against the old.]
PELL: Nell's fishing tomorrow night. Planks are in. She says she's earned one more.
BRAM: She'll have earned it. She's earned everything twice. That's Garrow.
SERA: Tell her the table's thirty-one off. Tell her it was nineteen two days ago.
BRAM: She knows, Sera. Whole slip knows. It's chalked on the post.
SERA: Then tell her it's moving.
BRAM: I'll tell her. She'll say "done". She says "done" to weather.
PELL: She said "done" at her own wedding. Halfway through. Then she stayed for the cake.
SERA: Keeper. Three o'clock. Don't forget.
KEEPER: Three o'clock.
SERA: Write the minutes. Not "early". Not "a bit". The minutes.
[Skip summary: On the slip, Sera times the morning tide thirty-one minutes ahead of her own table and stands in the water to read it. Pell jokes about the council's new "weather" notice. Sera asks the keeper to read the breakwater gauge every night at three and write it in minutes against her table. Bram coaxes her up one step. Nell plans to fish tomorrow night.]

### Scene 2 — The lamp room, dusk
[The lamp room at dusk. The chalk wall is full except for the bottom course of stone by the door. In it, very small: 19. Sera kneels there with the sextant. The Keeper holds the chalk.]
[From the stair, a voice is counting, fast.]
[Objective: Hold the chalk.]
SERA: Thirty-two. Same as last night. Mark it small.
[The Keeper marks it. Sera rubs the edge of it with her thumb until it is smaller.]
SERA: And the water. Thirty-one. Beside it.
KEEPER: Two numbers on one stone?
SERA: It's a big stone. For the size I write.
KEEPER: The Belt hasn't moved.
SERA: Not since the bell post. Three nights. Still is a number. Write it.
WREN: Ninety-eight! Ninety-nine! A hundred and... a hundred and four!
[Wren comes up the last turn in Bram's coat, sleeves rolled three times, a pot hugged to her chest.]
WREN: A hundred and four! Sera, it was a hundred and four! Is that more than yesterday?
SERA: It's a hundred and eight. It's always a hundred and eight.
WREN: Not when I count it! When I count it, it's what I count!
WREN: Keeper! Bram says the water came early! Is it early for supper too? Is it hungry?
KEEPER: Thirty-one minutes early.
WREN: What's a minute? Is a minute a small hour? Is it a baby one?
SERA: Sixty of them is an hour.
WREN: Sixty babies! That's a lot of babies for one hour!
WREN: Is thirty-one a lot of babies? Is that why Sera's cross?
SERA: I'm not cross. I'm counting.
[Bram comes up behind Wren with the lantern and a second pot.]
BRAM: She's always counting. Cross is just her counting face. Stew. Two pots.
BRAM: One for here, one for the road. Keeper, the road one's ours.
SERA: Patrol.
BRAM: Patrol. Mags'll have the pan book open. We're in it, under "road".
BRAM: Nobody's ever been in the pan book under "road". I'm very proud.
WREN: Can I patrol? I can walk a road. I'm good at roads. I did the whole stair.
BRAM: Not tonight, love. Tonight you patrol Sera. Make sure she eats.
WREN: How do I patrol eating?
BRAM: You sit next to her with a spoon and look sad.
WREN: I can't look sad. I've tried. My face won't go.
BRAM: Then look hungry. It works on Hester.
[Wren sits beside Sera with a spoon held up and a very hopeful face.]
SERA: That won't work.
WREN: Is it working a bit?
[Sera takes the spoon. She eats one mouthful without looking away from the wall.]
WREN: Bram! It worked a bit!
BRAM: That's one more than I get. You're promoted.
WREN: Keeper, do you count too? At the bottom, in your light?
KEEPER: The water. At three.
WREN: Three in the night? Is three awake then? I've never met a three in the night.
KEEPER: It's dark. And wet.
WREN: Can I come? I'll be very quiet. I'll be quieter than the water.
BRAM: The water's not quiet, love. It slaps.
WREN: Then I'll be quieter than a slap. That's easy. Slaps are loud.
SERA: She sleeps at three. Everyone with sense sleeps at three.
BRAM: Says the woman counting stars at midnight.
SERA: Midnight's not three.
[Bram looks at the stone. Thirty-two. Thirty-one. Small.]
BRAM: Sera. The water.
SERA: What about it?
BRAM: Thirty-one. Two days ago it was nineteen. You'll be standing in it to the knees by the end of the week.
SERA: Then I'll read it at the knees.
BRAM: That's what I was afraid you'd say.
SERA: Then you shouldn't have asked.
[Bram laughs, but he takes his time hanging the lantern. He trims the wick before he picks it up again.]
BRAM: Eyes up there, feet down here. Deal?
SERA: Deal. Stay in the light.
BRAM: Keeper. Road.
[Objective: Walk the crater road.]
[Skip summary: In the lamp room at dusk, the Belt holds at thirty-two and Sera chalks it beside the morning's thirty-one minutes, both very small in the last course. ### Scene 3 — The crater road, dusk
[The pan-house lane at the edge of dark. Mags stands on the step with her lamp up and the pan book open on her arm, pencil behind her ear.]
[Objective: Walk the crater road to the rim and back.]
MAGS: Road-walkers. Names.
BRAM: Bram Hollis. And the keeper.
MAGS: Bram Hollis. Keeper. Under road. Two.
[She taps the pencil on her pay-tin.]
MAGS: Back by dark or I'm coming after you with the tin. My feet don't want to. They'll do it.
BRAM: Anything on the road tonight, Mags?
MAGS: Abel says the reeds leaned at four. He says that most days. Then he says it again.
MAGS: Corra's in. Dunn boys are in. All my lot's in. You're the only names still out.
BRAM: That's a comfort. I think.
MAGS: Corra wanted to come. Under road. Says she can walk a road as good as any keeper.
BRAM: She can. She walked out of fifteen hounds with a week of bread in her apron.
MAGS: Then she can walk it in daylight and pick glass while she's at it. Fair's fair.
MAGS: That glass money's the first full tin I've had since the bad year, Bram Hollis.
MAGS: Abel says it's the marsh's. My feet say it's bread. My feet usually win.
BRAM: Your feet are the most sensible thing on this marsh, Mags.
MAGS: Tell them that. They never hear it from anyone.
[The crater road runs north between reed beds. Haze lies low over the marsh, and the crater is a pale bowl at the end of it.]
[Halfway up, Bram stops. Two tall, thin shapes stand at the edge of the road, faceless, very still. A third stands in the reeds beyond. They do not move.]
BRAM: Keeper. Three. Two on the verge, one in the reeds.
KEEPER: Standing.
BRAM: Standing. They're very good at standing. I've never seen one sit.
BRAM: Abel says leave them be if they leave you be. Abel says a lot.
KEEPER: The road goes past them.
BRAM: So. Funny thing about the road.
BRAM: It goes between them and the hole. Every step of it.
[He looks at the reeds. He looks back down the lane at Mags's lamp.]
BRAM: We could go round by the channel. It's wet. It's long. My boots would hate it.
KEEPER: The road's the job.
BRAM: The road's the job. That's what I said to Mags. I was hoping you'd forgotten.
BRAM: Right. Lamp up. Walk normal. Don't stare, it's rude.
[They walk. The lantern's light slides across the nearest figure's legs. Nothing.]
[They step between the figures and the crater. All three heads turn at once, without a sound.]
BRAM: That's not normal. That's them looking.
BRAM: Behind me. Behind the lamp. I'm not asking.
[Battle — Stage 2-1-2: The Crater Road. Enemies: Plumbs x3. Win condition: reach the rim and walk back to the pan-house lane. Special: a Plumb turns when a squad member crosses between it and the crater.]
[The first Plumb steps onto the road. It lifts one long arm and brings it down. Bram's lantern flares and the blow skids off the light.]
BRAM: That's eleven years of wick you're hitting. You'll get tired before it does.
[The Keeper strikes low, across the legs. The Plumb rocks. It doesn't fall.]
BRAM: Low's right! Low worked on the dogs! Keep at it!
[The third Plumb comes out of the reeds. It doesn't hurry. It walks straight, as if the reeds aren't there.]
BRAM: Don't let it get round. If it gets round, it's between us and Mags.
BRAM: And Mags'll have words. With all three of us.
[An arm comes down across the light, hard. Bram's boots slide back a foot in the ruts.]
BRAM: Keeper. If one gets past me, you don't go after it. You go back to Mags.
KEEPER: And you?
BRAM: I'm the lamp. Lamps stay where they're put. That's the joke of them.
[The Keeper drives the first back into the haze. It steps up out of the road and is gone, the way weather goes.]
[The other two stop. Then they step back, both at once, and go up after it.]
[The road is empty. Haze on the reeds. The crater sits pale at the end of it, the same as every night.]
BRAM: Rim. We go to the rim. Then we go back. That's the job.
[They walk to the rim. Bram holds the lantern over the glass bowl. It is quiet and faintly warm. Nothing climbs out.]
BRAM: There. Hole's still a hole. I'll tell Mags.
BRAM: She was right there, you know. Middle. Curled up like a cat on a warm step.
BRAM: I came over this lip like a sack of coal. Couldn't stop. Landed on my seat.
BRAM: She looked up at me like I was the strange one. Size of a bread bin.
[He looks at the middle of the bowl a moment longer. Then he turns away from it.]
[He turns for the lane and doesn't speak again for twenty steps.]
KEEPER: Bram?
BRAM: Hm.
KEEPER: You're quiet.
BRAM: I'm walking. You can't walk and talk on this road. It's a rule.
BRAM: I just made it.
[At the end of the lane, Mags's lamp is still up.]
MAGS: Bram Hollis. Keeper. Two out, two back. Fair's fair.
MAGS: You look like you've seen your own funeral, Bram Hollis.
BRAM: Just Plumbs, Mags. Three. They went up.
MAGS: Then go home and eat, the pair of you. I've counted you. That's my part done.

Bram's lantern holds them off and they go back up into the haze. Bram goes quiet on the walk back. Mags counts them in.]

### Scene 4 — The breakwater, night
[The breakwater at night. The lamp burns on its tower. Inside the end of the breakwater, a tall painted post stands in the water, marked in feet. The Keeper sits on the old keeper's stool by the lamp-house door with the logbook. Bram leans on the parapet with the road pot, eating out of it.]
[Objective: Read the gauge.]
BRAM: You're on the old man's stool. Flint's.
KEEPER: Jory said.
BRAM: He would. He used to sit under it. I used to see him from the tower window, a little lump by the door.
BRAM: Eat some of this. It's road stew. It's colder than house stew. Same stew.
KEEPER: You haven't said anything about the road.
BRAM: What's to say? Road was there. Hole was there. Three of them were there.
BRAM: Now they're not, and I'm here, and the stew's cold. That's the report.
KEEPER: They turned all at once.
BRAM: I know they did. I was the one in front.
[He puts the pot down on the parapet.]
BRAM: Show me this stick of Sera's, then. I've lived by this harbor forty years and never once read it.
KEEPER: You've never read the gauge?
BRAM: I've never had to. My grandad read the water by the color of it. Said it was greener at the top of the tide.
BRAM: Course, he said beer was greener at the top too.
[The Keeper takes the lantern to the steps and holds it out over the painted post. The water stands just under a white line.]
KEEPER: It's under the eleven.
BRAM: And that means?
KEEPER: Sera's table has the hour the water should stand at eleven feet. I look it up. Then I count back.
BRAM: Do it, then. Out loud. I like watching people do sums. It's restful when it's not me.
[The Keeper runs a finger down Sera's printed table by lantern light.]
KEEPER: The table says eleven feet at twenty to eleven. It's ten past ten.
BRAM: So it's half an hour early. Near enough.
KEEPER: Thirty minutes.
BRAM: She'd have your hide for "near enough". Write thirty.
KEEPER: Thirty-one this morning. Thirty tonight.
BRAM: That's one better, then. It's coming back.
KEEPER: Or it's one minute.
BRAM: Don't. You sound like her. One of her's plenty.
[Choice a: "It's one minute. That's all it is."]
[Choice b: "It's coming back, then."]
[Choices converge.]
BRAM: There you are. Either way it goes in the book.
[The Keeper writes it. Bram looks out over the harbor mouth. Past the end of the breakwater, the swell lifts white over a long low line in the dark.]
BRAM: See that? The white? That's the bar.
BRAM: Sand and shingle right across the harbor mouth. At low water a gull could stand on it.
BRAM: There's one way through. The gut. Deeper than the rest. It runs straight on this lamp.
KEEPER: Straight on the lamp?
BRAM: Built on it. When they put this light up, they put it where the old tower's line was.
BRAM: Before that it was us. Every boat that ever came through that gut came through on a Hollis.
BRAM: Now it's you. Funny old thing.
KEEPER: Do you mind?
BRAM: Me? No. I'd mind if it was dark.
[He picks the pot up again, scrapes the bottom, and puts the spoon in his pocket.]
BRAM: Keeper. Remember what I asked you? The first week.
KEEPER: Bad news straight.
BRAM: Bad news straight. You tell me straight, I tell her sideways. That's still the deal.
BRAM: She'll take it from me sideways. She'd go through a wall if it came at her straight.
KEEPER: What counts as bad news?
BRAM: Anything that'll put her in the water past the knees.
[He picks up the lantern.]
BRAM: I'll go up. Watch for the dips. Two, from the window. Means "seen you."
BRAM: You don't have to answer. My dad never needed answering. He just liked to say it.
[He goes back along the stones. Some minutes later, high on the headland, a small light in the tower window dips twice: down, up, down, up.]
[Skip summary: On the breakwater, Bram watches the keeper read the gauge against Sera's table for the first time: thirty minutes early. He points out the bar and the gut, the one channel through, which runs straight on the breakwater lamp, as it once ran on the old tower. He reminds the keeper of their deal: bad news straight to him, so he can tell Sera sideways.]

### Scene 5 — The tower stair, morning
[The lighthouse stair, early on day 17. Grey light through the slit windows, the cloud low outside them. The Keeper climbs up from the night shift with the logbook. Coming down to meet them: Wren, one hand on the wall, counting out loud, and Bram behind her with two empty pots.]
[Objective: Go down to the slip.]
WREN: Twenty-nine! Thirty! Thirty-one! Keeper! You're on thirty-one! Stand still, you're a number!
KEEPER: Good morning.
WREN: Is it? Sera says it's cloudy. She went down already. She went down in the dark.
BRAM: She always goes down in the dark. She likes to be at the water before it is.
WREN: Can you be somewhere before water? Isn't water already everywhere?
BRAM: Not on the stair, love. Not yet. Keep counting, you'll lose your place.
WREN: Thirty-two. Keeper's in my way. Thirty-two and a keeper.
KEEPER: Thirty minutes last night. At three.
BRAM: Thirty. One better than the morning. I'll tell her. Sideways, with a bun.
WREN: Keeper, did you see the three? The three in the night? What does it look like?
KEEPER: Dark. Like the two. And the four.
WREN: Oh. I thought it'd be a bit lighter. Being in the middle.
BRAM: Nights don't work like that, love. They're dark all the way through. That's the bargain.
WREN: Thirty babies! The small hours! Is it the same babies as yesterday?
BRAM: Different babies, love. Every day's got its own.
[They go down together, Wren in front. Bram takes each step with a small grunt.]
BRAM: Forty years on this stair. My knees know every one of them by name.
WREN: What are their names?
BRAM: Ow, mostly. The ninth's called Ow-Ow.
WREN: Bram. When I'm big, can I do the dips? Proper ones? Like your dad?
BRAM: You already do the dips. You did them the first week. Window, lantern, two.
WREN: Those were small dips. I want the proper big ones. For the boats.
[Bram doesn't answer straight away. He shifts the pots to the other arm.]
BRAM: Proper ones, then. When you're big.
WREN: How big is big? Is it lantern big? Is it door big?
BRAM: Big enough to hold the lamp up. That's all big is, on a stair.
WREN: I can nearly hold it now. I held it with two hands. You saw.
BRAM: I saw. You held it very well. Don't grow on my account. Twelve. Eleven. Ten.
WREN: You're counting backwards! That's cheating! I'm the counter!
[They reach the ninth step. Wren stops on it and bobs to it, very politely.]
WREN: Good morning, Ow-Ow.
[It wobbles under her. She steps down off it. Then she stops, and looks up at the underside of the turn above, at the dark stone where nobody looks. A moment too long.]
KEEPER: Wren?
WREN: Nothing! A spider. A big one. Is a spider a kind of star? It's got all the legs.
BRAM: Spiders are a kind of spider, love. Come on. Sera'll have the water timed and be cross we missed it.
[At the foot of the stair Wren jumps the last step with both feet.]
WREN: A hundred and nine!
BRAM: A hundred and eight.
WREN: It was nine today! It grew in the night! Things can, you know. Stairs can.
BRAM: Stairs can't, love. Stairs are the one thing in this town that stay put.
[He says it lightly. He holds the door for her a little longer than he needs to.]

She asks Bram if she can do his father's two lantern dips for the boats when she's big; he says yes, when she's big, and goes quiet. On the ninth step she looks a moment too long at the underside of the stair, and says it's a spider.]

### Scene 6 — The slip, morning
[The next morning, day 17. Grey and close, the sky a lid of low cloud. Sera stands on the slip in water to her shins. Patience lies at the slip with her crew loading nets. Nell stands on her rail.]
[Objective: Read the morning water.]
SERA: Fifty-two.

PELL: Fifty-two. That's not half an hour, is it.

KEEPER: The gauge said thirty last night.
SERA: Then it's moved twenty-two minutes since ten o'clock. Write that too.
[Nell slaps a flat hand on Patience's rail. Her crew stop loading.]
NELL: Surveyor. What's the number?
SERA: Fifty-two.
NELL: In my language.
SERA: The water's here nearly an hour before my table says.

SERA: Then look at it from here.

NELL: And it was nine the day it fell. I can count, Vant. I've had a month of counting.
SERA: Then count where it's going.
NELL: Where's it going, then? Say it plain.
SERA: Nine. Twelve. Nineteen. Thirty-one. Fifty-two.
SERA: Every morning it's further. Every morning it's further by more.
NELL: That's numbers. That's not plain.
SERA: That is plain. That's as plain as I have.
PELL: She means it's getting worse quicker, Nell. I'll say it. I'm old. Nobody minds.
NELL: Nobody asked you either, Pell.
PELL: Nobody ever does. I just stand here being right in a hat.
[Nell looks at her a long moment. She takes out her pipe, fills it, and doesn't light it.]
NELL: Hollis! You're on the slip. Tell her.
[Bram looks up from a coil of line.]
BRAM: Tell her what, Garrow?
NELL: That I've been over that bar a thousand times. In fog. In gales. Drunk, once, at a wedding.
BRAM: She has. I was at the wedding. She steered better than the groom stood.
NELL: There.
BRAM: Sera's still right, though.
NELL: Hollis.
BRAM: I'm only saying. Eat something first. Then go out and be stubborn.
[At the end of the slip, a young man sits on a bollard with his collar up. He looks at the breakwater lamp before he speaks.]
JORY: Leaving at night, Skipper? That's how my family does it.
NELL: I'll leave at noon and wave, Flint. Same as always.
JORY: Coming back's the hard part. Ask anybody.
NELL: Nobody asked you.
JORY: No. Not my business.
[Nell turns her back on him. But she looks at the breakwater lamp, too, just once.]
NELL: Rook! Nets!

TAM: Done, Skipper. One hole was a cart. One was just a cart's wheel.
NELL: South's warm, Rook. Nets tear there too.
TAM: Then they won't need me, will they.
NELL: Haul.
[Tam passes the bollard on his way back up the slip. He stops by Jory.]
TAM: Your sleeve's going. Left elbow. I'll do it tonight.
JORY: You don't have to keep doing that.
TAM: I know. I want to see if it's the same hole as last week.
[Sera has gone back into the water, to her shins, watching the post.]
BRAM: Sera. Up a step.
SERA: It's still coming. I want to see where it stops.
BRAM: It stopped at fifty-two. You wrote it.
SERA: That's where it turned. I want to see how high it goes.
[Bram goes down into the water after her, boots and all, and stands beside her.]
BRAM: Then we'll both see. I'll be very wet and very interested.
SERA: Bram. Your boots.
BRAM: My boots have been in worse. My boots have been full of beach.

### Scene 7 — The smokehouse, noon
[Hester's smokehouse at noon: long and low and hot. Patience's crew sit along one bench. On the hearth shelf, a row of pots with names chalked on the lids. One says WREN.]

### Scene 8 — The lamp room, dusk
[The lamp room at dusk. Cloud lies over the whole sky, grey to the horizon. Sera stands at the glass with the sextant raised at nothing. Wren is wrapped in the coat under the window. Bram has the lantern on the cradle step and a pot beside it.]

### Scene 9 — The breakwater, late

SERA: Hm.

KEEPER: I'm in the window tonight.
SERA: You're on the step. It's lower. It'll do.

KEEPER: And the gap's still rising.
[Bram says nothing. The Keeper writes it on the clean leaf, under the hour: "Three o'clock. Gauge: water at the table's 5.40 mark. 2 h 40 ahead, still rising."]
[The pen stops. Then the Keeper writes one more line under it: "Tables not usable."]
[Bram leans over and reads it, upside down. He reads it twice.]
BRAM: It was two hours ten at eleven. She said.
KEEPER: Two hours ten at the turn.
BRAM: And now it's forty. In four hours.
KEEPER: Since the sky moved.
BRAM: Don't. I can count. I'm slow, but I get there.
[He is quiet for a long time. The water slaps the post.]
BRAM: You told me straight.
KEEPER: That was the deal.
BRAM: That was the deal. You tell me straight. I tell her sideways.
BRAM: There's no sideways on that one.
BRAM: You know what she'll do with that. She'll go and stand in it.
BRAM: Not to the knees. On the bar. In the dark. With a pole and a watch.
BRAM: And the water doesn't come when the table says anymore. You've just written it does what it likes.
[He reaches into his oilskin and takes out a clasp knife. He opens it. He holds out his other hand for the book.]
BRAM: Give it here.
[The Keeper doesn't move.]
BRAM: Keeper. Give it here. Please.
[The Keeper gives him the book. Bram opens it flat on the parapet, finds the leaf, and lays the blade along the stitching. He cuts it out in one slow stroke, close to the thread. He folds the leaf twice and puts it inside his oilskin, against his chest, and buttons the oilskin over it.]
[He gives the book back. The night's page is all there. The next page is clean.]
BRAM: There.
BRAM: Now it's one bad night and not a worse one.
KEEPER: She asked for the three o'clock.
BRAM: She asked for two books. She's got two books. One's a leaf short.
BRAM: I'll tell her. I will. Sideways. When there's a sideways.
BRAM: When she's had a week of sleep and a month of supper. When the water's settled.
BRAM: Just not tonight. Not with Garrow on the bar and the sky going and her chalk still in her hand.
BRAM: Say nothing. That's all I'm asking. Not lie. Just nothing.
[Choice a: "It's her number, Bram."]
[Choice b: Say nothing.]
[Choices converge.]
[The Keeper says nothing more. Bram looks at them a long moment, and nods, as if something has been agreed.]
BRAM: I know whose number it is.
BRAM: I know exactly whose number it is. That's why it's in my coat.
[He closes the knife and puts it away. His hand stays flat on the oilskin, over the folded leaf.]
BRAM: Funny thing about knives. You only remember you've got one when you shouldn't.
[He gets up, slowly, and picks up the lantern.]
BRAM: Drink your tea. Hester'll ask if you drank it. She can tell from your face.
[He goes back along the stones toward the headland. Halfway, he stops, and doesn't turn round. Then he goes on.]
[Skip summary: At three Bram brings the keeper tea. He saw Patience on the bar and the shift from the tower, and couldn't leave Wren. Sera, asleep at the wall, has said she'll stand on the bar every turn with a pole. The keeper reads the gauge: two hours forty minutes ahead of the table and still rising, and writes "Tables not usable." Bram says she'll go and stand in it. He cuts the leaf out of the log with his knife, puts it in his oilskin, and asks the keeper to say nothing.]

### Scene 15 — The lamp room, first light
[Grey first light on the salt-stained glass. The Keeper comes up the last turn of the stair. On the last course of the wall, very small: 19, 32, 31, 32, 52, and then, larger than the rest, 28, and beside it "2.10".]
[Sera is asleep sitting up against the wall, a blanket over her knees, chalk in her hand. Under the window, a long shape lies curled under Bram's coat, the coat pulled up over its head. It doesn't move.]
[Bram sits on the top step with his oilskin buttoned to the throat. He doesn't look round when the Keeper comes up.]
[Objective: Climb to the lamp room.]
BRAM: Lamp out?
KEEPER: At dawn. I wrote it.
BRAM: On the new page.
KEEPER: On the new page.
[A pause. Bram nods at nothing.]
BRAM: Sit. Mind the ninth. You'll have minded it.
BRAM: Did you drink the tea?
KEEPER: Some.
BRAM: Hester'll ask. She'll look at your face and know it was some.
BRAM: I'll say you drank it all. That's not a lie. That's catering.
[The Keeper sits on the step beside him. Neither of them looks at the shape under the window.]
KEEPER: Have you looked at her?
BRAM: No.
BRAM: She's breathing. I've listened. That's enough for this hour.
BRAM: I'll look when she's up. Not before.
KEEPER: The coat's over her head.
BRAM: She does that when it's cold. It's cold.
[He doesn't say anything else about it. His hand is flat on the oilskin.]
BRAM: Sera told the blanket something else. Before she went off.
KEEPER: What?
BRAM: "Sixteen. Then four." Like a sum. Then she was asleep.
BRAM: I can do that sum. I'm slow, but I can do it. I don't like where it goes.
KEEPER: Where does it go?
BRAM: Don't. I'm not asking you. I'm telling the step.
BRAM: Garrow's going. Tam said, on the slip. "When she's dry."
KEEPER: She said it to everyone.
BRAM: She's said it every season since we were twenty. This time she didn't slap anything.
BRAM: That's how you know with Nell. When she goes quiet about it, she's packed.
BRAM: Half the slip'll go with her. The good half. The half that can still steer.
[Down in the harbor, the water is already coming up the slips, well ahead of the tide table nailed beside them.]
[Sera stirs. Her eyes open halfway.]
SERA: Twenty-eight. Is it on?
BRAM: It's on. You put it on. In the dark, with your coat on.
SERA: I'll check it. When it's light.
BRAM: It's nearly light. Check it nearly.
SERA: The water. Morning turn. I want to time it.
[Bram looks out of the glass, down at the slips, at water that is already there.]
BRAM: Sleep, Sera.
BRAM: Water'll still be wet in an hour. It always is. That's the one thing it's good at.
SERA: That's not a number.
BRAM: It's a promise. It's better than a number. It's got a spoon in it.
[Sera's eyes close again. The chalk slips out of her fingers and rolls on the stone. Bram picks it up and puts it on the step beside her, where she'll find it.]
[Below, the water comes on up the slips. Nobody is there to time it.]
KEEPER: The water's coming in.
BRAM: I know.
KEEPER: She'll be cross.
BRAM: She's always cross. Cross is her counting face.
BRAM: Today she'll be cross and slept. That's one better.
KEEPER: You'll tell her. Sideways.
BRAM: I said I would. I'll find the sideways.
BRAM: There's always a sideways. You just have to walk far enough round.
[He looks at the shape under the coat. Just for a moment. Then he looks back at the wall.]
BRAM: Let her sleep. Let them both sleep.
BRAM: Let her sleep. Water'll still be wet in an hour.
[He keeps his hand on his oilskin, over the pocket, and doesn't take it off.]
[Objective: Return at dusk.]
[Skip summary: At first light the keeper climbs to the lamp room. Sera has chalked the shift on the wall and fallen asleep against it. Wren sleeps under the window with the coat over her head; Bram won't look until she's up. When Sera half wakes and asks to time the morning tide, Bram tells her the water will still be wet in an hour, and lets her sleep through the turn. His hand stays on his oilskin pocket.]

### Scene 16 — Coda: the breakwater, the next dusk
[Dusk, the next day. The breakwater. The lamp is not yet lit. Jory sits on his bollard at the far end with his collar up. The logbook hangs on the old keeper's nail by the lamp-house door.]
[Objective: Light the lamp.]
JORY: Keeper. Light's late.
KEEPER: It's not dusk yet.
JORY: Dusk on the west slip. Never mind. Not my business.
[He gets up. He crosses to the lamp-house and looks at the logbook on its nail. He doesn't touch it.]
JORY: Nell's planks are out on the slip. Every one. Drying.
JORY: When she's dry, she says. Whole harbor heard her.
KEEPER: You were there.
JORY: I was on the line. Doesn't make me anything.
JORY: Tam's shirt's still on the loft rail. He went in to the waist. Can't swim.
JORY: Says he'd do it again. He would, too. That's the trouble with Tam.
KEEPER: You went in too.
JORY: To the knees. Knees don't count.
JORY: Hester sent soup down to the slip at four. For the crews. Didn't write it down.
JORY: I watched her not write it. She's got a whole face for not writing things.
JORY: Can I read it? The book.
KEEPER: You've never asked.
JORY: Asking now. You can say no. It's the council's book.
[The Keeper takes the book off the nail and holds it out. Jory takes it in both hands, carefully, the way somebody takes a thing they were once not allowed to touch.]
JORY: He taught me to read it. The old keeper. Before I could read anything else.
JORY: Sundays, on the stool. Me on his knee and the book on mine.
JORY: Not the words first. You count the leaves first. By the stitching.
JORY: He said a book can lie by being short.
[He opens it at the back and runs his thumb along the inside of the spine, leaf by leaf, counting under his breath. His lips move.]
[He reaches last night. The full page: dusk, cloud, the turn, Patience on the bar, three boats in, the Belt at two.]
[His thumb stops on that page. He reads the line about the bar twice. Then his thumb goes on.]
[After it, close in by the stitching, there is a stub: a narrow edge of paper, cut clean. Then "Lamp out at dawn."]
[Jory's thumb stays on the stub.]
[He looks at it a long time. Then he looks up at the Keeper. The Keeper looks back.]
[Neither of them says anything.]
[Jory closes the book and hangs it back on its nail, square, the way it was.]
JORY: Book's sound. Mostly.
[He glances at the lamp, as if checking it's lit. It isn't yet.]
JORY: Go on, then. Light it.
[The Keeper lights the lamp. The wick catches. Jory watches it the whole time.]
JORY: Lit on time.
[He goes back to his bollard and sits with his collar up, looking at the light and nothing else.]
[Skip summary: At dusk the next day, Jory asks to read the breakwater log, the way the old keeper taught him: count the leaves by the stitching before reading the words. He reads the line about Patience on the bar, then finds a leaf cut out after it. He looks at the keeper, says nothing about it, and watches the lamp lit.]
