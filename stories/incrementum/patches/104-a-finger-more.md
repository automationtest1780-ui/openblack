## Planning sheet (gacha 14.2)

**Player fantasy:** you held a road in the dark beside four people while the whole town finally looked up.

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**
- **Sera** wants tonight's count unbroken, the same as every dusk since the fall. Obstacle: glass things on the marsh road, Bram wanting her inside the lantern's light, and a magistrate she won't speak to choosing where they stand. Cost: she breaks her deal with Bram ("Not tonight") and steps out of the light to count. The town sees the Belt move, and still only one person in it can tell her where. By morning the council has a new word, and the wall has one course left.
- **Bram** wants everyone home and fed. Obstacle: Sera out of the light, Wren walking toward the pack, a whole town with no supper. Cost: he goes quiet three times, gets angry at Sera for the first time, and in the morning rolls the coat sleeves three times.

## Script

### Scene 1 — The breakwater, dusk
[The new breakwater light, squat and bright against a red sky. The Keeper opens the logbook. Every page is the same line, night after night: "Lamp lit at dusk."]
[A young man sits on a bollard at the far end of the breakwater with his collar up. He is looking at the lamp, not the sea.]
[Objective: Light the lamp.]
JORY: Keeper. Light's late.
KEEPER: It's not dusk yet.
JORY: It's dusk on the west slip. Sun's off the water there already.
JORY: Not my business. Light it how you like.
[The Keeper lights the lamp. The wick catches. Jory watches it the whole time.]
JORY: You trim it too short. It'll gutter by two.
KEEPER: Show me?
JORY: No.
[He gets up anyway, crosses, and turns the wick up a quarter turn without touching anything else.]
JORY: There. Now it'll gutter by four. Better than two.
KEEPER: Who taught you?
JORY: The old keeper. He trimmed it every dusk, years and years. I held the matches.
JORY: Then the herring didn't come, and the wages didn't come, and we went.
[He glances at the lamp again, as if checking it's still lit.]
JORY: You write the wind? In the book?
KEEPER: Every night.
JORY: He wrote the wind too. Every night. Nobody read it.
JORY: Then one night somebody read it, and that was the end of us.
KEEPER: What did it say?
JORY: Does it matter? It said the light was lit.
[He doesn't say anything else about it. He nods up the harbor instead, toward the customs house.]
JORY: You've seen the notice? On the customs house door?
KEEPER: Fog-madness.
JORY: Fog-madness. Big letters. Magistrate pinned it up himself. Red ink.
JORY: Half the harbor's seen the tall things in the reeds. Now it's official that they haven't.
JORY: Old Pell read it out loud on the quay. Then he spat on the step.
JORY: Somebody had to scrub it. Funniest thing I've seen since I got off the boat.
KEEPER: Do you believe it? The notice?
JORY: I believe the council likes a word it can write down.
JORY: Took them two weeks to write my family down. "Gone south." Two words.
KEEPER: Why did you come back?
JORY: Tide was right.
[He says it to the lamp, not to the Keeper. Then he looks north, past the harbor, to the dark line of the marsh.]
JORY: You're going up the old light. To her.
KEEPER: Every dusk.
JORY: Town's noticed. Breakwater keeper, up the dead tower every night with the mad surveyor.
JORY: Hester says it's courting. Nell says it's sense. Tam says it's nice to have a light on up there.
JORY: I don't say anything. Not my business.
JORY: Go on, then. I'll watch it.
KEEPER: Why?
JORY: Not for you. Don't make a thing of it.
JORY: Somebody ought to watch it who knows how it gutters. That's all.
JORY: If the council comes asking where you were, I'll tell them haze. Everybody else does.
JORY: Go on. Your surveyor's lamp is already up. Look.
[High on the headland, a small light climbs window by window inside the dark tower.]
JORY: She never misses. Town hates that about her.
JORY: Town hated it about the old keeper, too.
[Objective: Climb to the lamp room.]
[Skip summary: At dusk on the breakwater, Jory Flint, the last keeper's son, fixes the keeper's wick and offers to watch the lamp. He won't say why he came back. The council's notice on the customs house calls the marsh sightings fog-madness.]

### Scene 2 — The lamp room, dusk
[The lamp room. The chalk wall is nearly covered. Sera kneels at the bottom of it with the sextant in one hand. Her newest marks are half the size of the old ones.]
[Bram sits on the top step with a covered pot. From far down the stair, a small voice is counting.]
SERA: Lamp room. Dusk. You're on time. Hold the chalk.
[The Keeper holds the chalk stub. Sera sights through the glass, over the headland.]
SERA: Thirty-six. Mark it there. Under the last one. Small.
[The Keeper marks it. Sera looks, and rubs the edge of it with her thumb until it's smaller.]
SERA: Smaller.
BRAM: You're writing smaller. I thought I'd say, before you're writing in ants.
SERA: The wall's the size it is.
BRAM: Walls usually are. That's the trouble with walls. They don't grow.
BRAM: There's more stone downstairs. A whole tower of it. I've oiled every hinge on it.
SERA: Down there's not where I sight from.
BRAM: No. It's where I live, mostly. Never mind.
KEEPER: How much is left?
SERA: The corner by the door. The bottom course, and a bit above it. The bit's tonight.
KEEPER: And after?
SERA: After, I'll find more.
[She says it the way she says a number. Nobody asks where.]
WREN: Eighty-nine! Ninety! Ninety-one!
[Wren comes up the last turn of the stair in Bram's coat, sleeves rolled four times, out of breath.]
WREN: Ninety-one! Bram, it's ninety-one tonight! Yesterday it was eighty-eight!
WREN: Did the stair grow? Can stairs do that? Is it because you oil it?
BRAM: It's the same stair, love. You're just counting with more feeling.
WREN: I counted with all my feet! Did you count it, Sera? Did you count me coming up?
SERA: I counted the Belt. Sit by the cradle. Don't stand in the window.
WREN: Because I'm blocking it? Is the Belt shy?
SERA: Because you're in my light.
[Wren sits by the empty lamp cradle, very fast. Then she gets up again.]
WREN: Keeper! You came up too! Did you count your stairs?
KEEPER: I lost count at the ninth.
WREN: The ninth's the grumpy one! Bram says it had a hard life! Did it grumble at you?
KEEPER: It wobbled.
WREN: That's how it says hello. Bram says that's manners, for a step.
BRAM: Fish and potato. Hester's fish, my potato, the stair's dust. Bowls, everyone.
SERA: After the count.
BRAM: The count's done. You said thirty-six. I heard you.
SERA: I say it twice. Once when I see it. Once when it's written.
BRAM: Then say it twice with a spoon in. It's a big mouth, it'll fit.
[Sera doesn't take the bowl. She chalks the number beside the Keeper's mark, small.]
SERA: Thirty-six.
WREN: Can I write one? I can do a three! It's two bumps. I practiced on the step.
SERA: No.
WREN: Can I do the six, then? The six is just a three that fell over.
SERA: The wall's for counts. Not practice.
[Wren looks at the wall, then at her own hands. She sits on them.]
KEEPER: Does anyone read it? Besides you?
SERA: The council read me once. Then they wrote haze.
SERA: Now they've written fog-madness. That's two words for one thing. That's how you know they're lying.
KEEPER: Maybe they just don't know.
SERA: Then they could write that. "Don't know." It's shorter.
SERA: If you want to argue it, take it down to the customs house. They like visitors.
BRAM: Sera.
SERA: I'm not shouting. I'm counting.
[She turns back to the wall. The conversation is over. Bram looks at the Keeper and shrugs, the way a man shrugs about weather.]
BRAM: She's like this every night. You should see her at breakfast. Oh, wait, you can't. She doesn't eat it.
BRAM: Don't take it to heart. She shut me out for a month when I first came up.
BRAM: I just kept bringing soup till I was furniture.
[Wren is at the glass now, nose against it, looking north over the dark marsh.]
WREN: Sera? The crater's sparkly.
SERA: It's glass. Glass catches the last light.
WREN: But it's moving sparkly. Is it meant to move? Is that a thing glass does at suppertime?
[Sera lifts the sextant north. She holds it there.]
SERA: Bram.
BRAM: I'm eating. Whatever it is, it can wait for potato.
SERA: Bram. Come and look.
[Bram puts the bowl down. He goes to the glass. For a moment he says nothing at all.]
SERA: Something's climbing out of the bowl. Over the lip. Low.
SERA: Four. Seven. Eight. Four legs each.
WREN: Are they coming to visit? Do they know we're up here? Should I wave?
SERA: They're on the marsh road. Heading south.
BRAM: So. Funny thing about the marsh road.
BRAM: It goes straight past the pans. And Mags has twenty people on those pans at dusk.
[Bram stands. He takes the storm lantern off its hook and checks the wick by feel.]
BRAM: Right. Wren, coat buttoned. Keeper, you're with me. Sera—
SERA: I'm coming. I'll count from the road.
BRAM: You'll count from behind my lamp, is where you'll count.
SERA: Chalk, Keeper. Bring it.
[Objective: Reach the salt pans.]
[Skip summary: In the lamp room at dusk, Sera chalks the Belt at thirty-six, writing small because the wall is nearly full, and shuts the keeper out when they question the council. Wren spots the crater sparkling. Through her sextant, Sera sees four-legged shapes climbing out of the crater and heading down the marsh road toward the salt pans.]

### Scene 3 — The pan-house lane
[The salt pans at the edge of dark. Square white beds, a low pan-house, a rutted lane out to the marsh road. A woman stands on the pan-house step with a lamp held high, shouting names.]
[Objective: Find the pan folk.]
MAGS: Abel! The Dunn boys! Ferris! Me!
MAGS: Corra! Corra Vey!
[Silence from the pans. An old man with a reed-hook leads a shaggy pony up to the step.]
ABEL: She's not in, Mags. She went back for an evening pick.
MAGS: I told her dawn. Dawn picks. Who picks glass in the dark?
ABEL: Someone who's counted what a handful buys.
BRAM: Mags! How many have you got?
MAGS: Bram Hollis. Thank the tides. Everyone but one. Everyone but Corra.
MAGS: My feet have run the whole lane twice. They want a word with her.
SERA: Where was she picking?
MAGS: North pan. Nearest the crater. Where else would she be.
MAGS: That girl'd pick glass off a sinking boat if it paid.
[Footsteps on the marsh road, coming from the town side. A tall man in a magistrate's coat walks out of the dusk, gloved, alone.]
BRAM: Magistrate! Evening walk again?
IVO: From the customs house window one sees a great deal of marsh, Hollis. I came to see the rest of it.
BRAM: No lamp again. One of these nights you'll walk into a channel.
IVO: One of these nights I will. Not tonight. Tonight you have a lamp.
[Ivo's eyes go to the Keeper and stay there a moment.]
IVO: Keeper. We keep meeting on this road.

### Scene 13 — The customs house

IVO: When I look at that child, Keeper, I see a cost I cannot count yet.

KEEPER: She's a kid in a coat.
IVO: That is what Hollis says. Hollis is usually right about people.
IVO: I am usually right about sums. We have never yet had to find out which matters more.
[He turns from the window. He picks up the pen Gage left, and pulls a clean sheet toward him.]
IVO: Take her up to Hollis. Take the pie, too. I find I am not hungry.
IVO: And Keeper. If the surveyor asks what I said tonight—
[He stops. He looks at the clean sheet.]
IVO: No. She will not ask. Tell her nothing. She would not want it from me.
[Skip summary: At the customs house, the keeper brings Ivo a pie from Mags and counts him. Councillor Gage has spent an hour proposing words for the notice, and Ivo has struck out six: a frightened town drowns faster than a fooled one. Ivo promises a word by breakfast. Watching Wren asleep and scratching, he tells the keeper she is a cost he can't count yet.]

### Scene 14 — The lamp room, late
[The lamp room, late. One lamp on the cradle step. Sera kneels at the bottom of the wall with Pell's tag beside her, writing very small. Bram sits on the top step. Beside Sera, a pot with BRAM chalked on the lid sits unopened.]
[The Keeper comes up the last turn carrying Wren, half asleep, with a bit of pie in each hand. She curls up under the window in Bram's coat almost before she's set down.]
[Objective: Bring the counts up.]
SERA: Keeper. Hand.
[The Keeper holds out their hand: 9, 36, and 32 under it, smudged. Sera copies the numbers onto the wall.]
SERA: Your count. Mine. Pell's.
SERA: Thirty-six at dusk. Thirty-two at dark. Three counts that agree.
KEEPER: Same as night three.
SERA: Same as night three. Three counts. Same four degrees.
SERA: The only difference is a whole town watched this time.
[She sits back on her heels and looks at the wall. There is very little clean stone left: the bottom course, low by the door.]
SERA: And not one of them can say where it was.
KEEPER: Pell could.
SERA: Pell's been counting for forty years. He just calls it pots.
SERA: The rest saw a string of beads. You can't put beads on a wall.
KEEPER: Does it matter? That they saw it?
SERA: It matters tonight.
SERA: In a week, half of them will say it went west. In a month, they'll say it was a cloud.
SERA: By spring, they'll say they never saw it, and they'll be sure.
SERA: The wall won't say it was a cloud.
[She chalks Pell's bearing beside the other two. Then, without looking round:]
SERA: Did the magistrate say anything?
KEEPER: He said you wouldn't ask.
[Sera's chalk stops. A long pause.]
SERA: He's right. I didn't.
[She goes back to writing, as if the question was never asked.]
[Bram leans over from the step and takes the lid off the pot.]
BRAM: Stew. My name's on the lid, so it's mine to give away. Eat.
SERA: In a minute.
BRAM: You said in a minute when I came up. You said it at dusk. That's six hours of minutes.
[Sera doesn't move. Bram settles back on the step.]
BRAM: They saw it, Sera. Every one of them.
SERA: I know.
BRAM: You were right in front of the whole harbor. Nell said it out loud. Nell. On a barrel.
SERA: She said I was right. Then she asked me where it was.
SERA: They saw it, and they wanted me to tell them what it was. Like the council does. Like a story.
SERA: Seeing isn't counting. By market day it's beads on a string. By winter it's a bit of weather.
BRAM: Then I'll tell it right. I'm good with stories.
SERA: You'll tell it with a joke in.
BRAM: Only at the front. That's where the jokes go.
[A pause. Bram looks at Wren under the window. In her sleep, she is scratching at her arm through the coat sleeve.]
[Bram goes quiet. He watches her for a long moment.]
SERA: What?
BRAM: Nothing. She's itchy. Hester's pie, probably. Too much pepper.
[Sera looks at Wren too. She looks for longer than Bram does. Then she turns back to the wall.]
SERA: You were angry. On the bridge.
BRAM: I was.
SERA: You've never been angry at me.
BRAM: I have. I just eat it, usually. Like stew.
SERA: Why not tonight?
BRAM: Because you stood out of the light with your back to fifteen glass dogs.
BRAM: And I couldn't follow you out there, because then nobody's holding the lamp.
BRAM: That's all. That's the whole of it. Eat your stew.
[Sera looks at the stew. She picks up the spoon. She eats.]
[Bram watches, as if it's a thing he's never seen.]
BRAM: ...Well.
SERA: It's hot.
BRAM: It's meant to be hot. That's the trick of it. Most nights it isn't, by the time you get to it.
SERA: I came into the light. At the bend.
BRAM: I saw.
SERA: I could see the chimney from there.
BRAM: I know. He told you so.
SERA: I worked it out. He only said it first.
SERA: He said it like he'd stood there before. Counting.
BRAM: He's stood everywhere in this town before. He's been magistrate twenty years.
SERA: Hm.
[She writes nothing. She just looks at the wall a moment longer.]
[Bram laughs, once, very quietly, so as not to wake Wren.]
BRAM: Eyes up there, feet down here. Deal?
SERA: Deal. Stay in the light.
BRAM: I'll stay. I'm the one holding it.
[Skip summary: Late in the lamp room, Sera copies the keeper's hand and Pell's tag onto the wall: three counts, four degrees, the same as night three. One row of clean stone is left. She says the town saw it but nobody counted it, and that the wall won't forget. Bram watches Wren scratch her arm in her sleep and blames the pepper. He tells Sera why he was angry on the bridge. She eats the stew while it's hot, and they make their deal again.]

### Scene 15 — The breakwater, before dawn
[The breakwater in the grey before dawn. The lamp is guttering low. Jory sits against the lamp housing with his knees up and his collar higher. He hasn't moved all night.]
[Objective: Write up the night.]
JORY: Four o'clock. Guttered at four. Told you.
KEEPER: You stayed all night.
JORY: Said I would. Doesn't make it a thing.
[He gets up stiffly and turns the wick down to save the oil, without being asked.]
JORY: Wind nil all night. Two boats in late off the north banks.
JORY: One went out at three for the crab pots, and came back in at half past without them.
JORY: Said he couldn't find the marks. Said they'd all moved. Wasn't drunk, either. I checked.
KEEPER: You didn't write it in the book?
JORY: Not my book.
[He holds out the logbook. The Keeper takes it.]
JORY: You saw it from the marsh road?
KEEPER: Sera counted it. Thirty-six to thirty-two.
JORY: I saw it from here. It went over the bell post. Whole harbor screamed. Then whole harbor shut up.
JORY: Not one of them looked at the lamp. Lamp was fine.
JORY: Lamp was fine all night. I'll swear to that, if anybody asks. Nobody'll ask.
[The Keeper opens the book at tonight's page. The pen hovers.]
KEEPER: What do I write?
JORY: Whatever's true. That's the whole job. The old keeper said that.
JORY: He wrote what was true, one night. Light lit. Tide came in. Town read it and said he was lying.
JORY: Don't know why I told you that. Not my business.
[The Keeper writes. "Lamp lit at dusk. Wind nil. The Belt moved east at full dark, seen from the marsh road and from this light."]
JORY: That's more than one line.
KEEPER: It was more than one thing.
JORY: Suits you.
JORY: Council'll read that, you know. Not tomorrow. Some day. Somebody always does.
KEEPER: Let them.
JORY: That's what he said.
[He says nothing for a while. The water slaps the breakwater stones. The tide is high already, higher than it ought to be at this hour.]
JORY: Tam came out here at two. Brought me a heel of bread and a blanket with a hole in it.
JORY: Said he'd mend the hole tomorrow. Said he dropped a stitch tonight, first time since he was nine.
JORY: He was more upset about the stitch than the sky. That's Tam.
KEEPER: Are you upset?
JORY: About the sky? It's not my sky.
JORY: Course, it's not anybody else's either. That's the funny thing. I thought I'd mind more, coming back. Turns out it's everybody's problem.
[He looks back at the lamp, once, as if checking it's still lit. Then he pulls a folded ticket half out of his pocket, looks at it, and puts it back.]
JORY: I'm going to Hester's. She'll have kept pie. She keeps everything.
KEEPER: Thank you, Jory.
[A pause before he answers. It's a long one.]
JORY: ...Don't make a thing of it.
JORY: Go on. Your surveyor'll be wanting you. Mind the ninth step. I hear it's had a hard life.
[Objective: Climb to the lamp room.]
[Skip summary: Before dawn at the breakwater, Jory has watched the keeper's lamp all night. He reports a crab boat that couldn't find its marks, and says the lamp was fine all night though nobody looked at it. He tells the keeper the old keeper once wrote what was true and the town called him a liar. The keeper writes the shift into the log.]

### Scene 16 — The lamp room, morning
[Grey morning on the glass. The Keeper comes up the last turn of the stair. Sera is asleep sitting up against the wall, chalk in her hand. Bram is gone; his lantern still burns on the cradle step.]
[Under the window, Wren stirs, sits up, and stretches. Both her hands come all the way out of the coat sleeves.]
WREN: Keeper! Keeper, look! My hands are out!
WREN: The coat's let go of them. Did the coat get smaller in the night? Is it that kind of wool?
KEEPER: Stand up.
[Wren stands up. The coat's hem, which dragged on the floor yesterday, hangs above her ankles. Her head is higher on the glass than it was at dusk. Not much. Two fingers.]
WREN: Am I standing taller? Is that a thing you can do by accident?
WREN: I didn't do it on purpose. Is it bad on purpose? Is it only bad on purpose?
KEEPER: Does it hurt?
WREN: No! It itched. Now it doesn't. Is that how itching ends? It just stops and you're taller?
[She goes to the glass and stands on tiptoe, then flat-footed, then on tiptoe again, comparing her reflection with the window frame.]
WREN: Yesterday I was the second bar. Look. Now I'm nearly the third. Did the window go down?
KEEPER: I don't think windows go down.
WREN: Stairs grow. You heard. Ninety-one. Maybe windows shrink. Maybe everything's moving and I'm the only one still.
WREN: Is that it? Am I the still one? That'd be funny, wouldn't it?
[She laughs. The Keeper doesn't. She notices, and stops.]
WREN: Is it not funny? I thought it was funny.
[Footsteps on the stair. Bram comes up with a loaf under one arm and a paper in his hand.]
BRAM: Bread, fresh, and news, stale. Customs house has a new notice up—
[He sees Wren. He stops on the top step.]
WREN: Bram! Look! My hands came out!
[Bram puts the loaf down on the cradle step. Very carefully, as if it might break.]
BRAM: So they did.
[He kneels in front of her. He takes the left sleeve and rolls it. Once. Twice. Three times. Her hand is out. He stops.]
[He does the right. Three times. He stops.]
WREN: Three! Is three good? Is three better than four?
[Bram doesn't answer. He keeps hold of the cuff a little longer than he needs to.]
[Sera is awake now, watching from the wall. She looks at Wren the way she looks at the Belt.]
SERA: She's taller.
BRAM: Don't.
SERA: Two fingers. I'm only saying what's there.
BRAM: I know what's there. Don't.
[Sera's hand lifts, with the chalk in it, toward the wall. It stops. She puts the chalk down.]
WREN: Bram? Did I do something? Is it bad?
BRAM: No, love.
BRAM: Three's grand. Three's a good number. Ask Sera, she loves a number.
WREN: Sera? Is three a good number?
SERA: It's exact.
WREN: Exact! That's better than a lot! You said!
[She bounces once in the too-short coat. Then she looks at the underside of the stair, a moment too long, before she turns back.]
KEEPER: What's the notice say?
BRAM: Oh. That.
[He unfolds the paper. It's a copy, in plain black letters.]
BRAM: "Weather. The council is watching it." Ivo's words. Black ink, this time.
SERA: Weather.
BRAM: Not haze. Not fog-madness. Weather. It's something, Sera.
SERA: It's one word for it. Yesterday it was two.
BRAM: And "watching." The council's never watched anything in its life. It's never had to.
SERA: He picked "watching" so they'd stop.
[She looks out through the glass, down at the customs house roof in the grey light.]
SERA: He's moved a finger too.
[Bram folds the paper and puts it in his coat. He picks the loaf back up and starts to tear it.]
BRAM: Right. Here's what's happening. Tonight, supper. Up here. A proper one, sat down.
BRAM: Pie. Hester's crust. I'll carry the oven up the stair if I have to.
SERA: Not him.
BRAM: I never said him.
SERA: You were going to.
BRAM: I was going to say pie. Pie's all I said. Here. Bread.
[Wren takes the bread with both hands, free of the sleeves, and looks at them as if they're new.]
WREN: My hands can hold the whole bread now. Look. Both ends.
BRAM: So they can, love.
[Bram goes down the stair. He doesn't joke on the way. Halfway down, he starts whistling, too loud.]
WREN: Keeper. Bram's whistling.
KEEPER: Is that bad?
WREN: He whistles when he's carrying something heavy. Is the bread heavy? It wasn't heavy for me.
KEEPER: Maybe it's the pie he's thinking about.
WREN: Maybe. Pie's very heavy. I'll help him carry it tonight. My hands are bigger now. Did you see?
[Sera goes to the wall. She kneels by the door, at the bottom course: one clean course of stone, and nothing else.]
SERA: One course left. I'll write small.
KEEPER: And then?
SERA: Then I'll write smaller.
KEEPER: And then?
SERA: You ask a lot of "and then" for a keeper.
SERA: Then I'll find more wall. There's always more wall. Bram says there's a whole tower of it.
[She doesn't sound sure. She runs her thumb along the last clean course, stone by stone, as if counting it by touch.]
SERA: You were on the road last night. You saw it go from the chimney to the bell post.
KEEPER: I saw it.
SERA: In a month, will you still say the chimney? Not "east". Not "a bit". The chimney.
[Choice a: "The chimney."]
[Choice b: "It's on my hand. And in my book."]
[Choices converge.]
SERA: Good.
SERA: Then that's two of us. In a month.
[She stands and looks down at the harbor. The morning tide is already at the slips, lapping one step higher than the tide table nailed beside them.]
SERA: Tide's in early.
SERA: Eleven minutes early.
KEEPER: Is that a lot?
SERA: It's eleven minutes.
SERA: Hold the chalk.
[The Keeper holds the chalk. Sera writes "11" in the last clean course, very small.]
[Objective: Return at dusk. Supper in the lamp room.]
[Skip summary: In the morning Wren's hands are out of the coat sleeves; she has grown two fingers in the night. Bram rolls the sleeves three times, not four, and goes quiet. Sera starts to mark it and stops. The council's new notice says "Weather. The council is watching it." Bram plans a proper supper in the lamp room tonight, not for Ivo. One course of clean stone is left, and Sera chalks the morning tide in it: eleven minutes early.]
