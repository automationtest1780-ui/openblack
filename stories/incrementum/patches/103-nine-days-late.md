**Player fantasy:** you're the only council coat the marsh trusts, and the one person who sees the magistrate when he thinks nobody is looking.

**Featured heroes: want / obstacle / cost**
- **Ivo** wants nobody in Saltreach to call the glass anything but glass. Obstacle: a stranger steps off the packet already knowing there's glass to buy, and nine days late. Cost: he lies on the slip with Sera listening, then walks out to look at the thing he denied, and the keeper sees him do it.

**New locked facts (for the ledger)**

- Abel asked the magistrate in public to fill the crater. Ivo said nothing fell, there was haze and a hot night, and the council won't pay to bury glass.
- Sera paced the crater at noon: thirty-two paces rim to rim. She said it aloud on the slip, through the keeper; Ivo heard it and didn't write it down.
- Teodor Lisle stepped off the packet on the ninth day. He paid Corra a shilling for a piece offered at a penny and never haggles. Mags sold him the pan-house half-share.

## Script

### Scene 1 — The breakwater, dawn
[Grey dawn over the anchorage. The breakwater lamp still burns. The Keeper writes in the logbook: "Ninth morning since the fall. Wind light, north-east. Two boats out."]
[Two herring boats slide out past the light. A girl of about seventeen runs along the breakwater stones with a full apron, shouting after them.]
[Objective: Talk to the glass-picker.]
CORRA: Luck for the boat! Penny a piece! Fell out of the sky, didn't it?
CORRA: Can't get luckier than that! Penny! Come on, I've seen your purse!
[A deckhand flips a coin across the gap. Corra catches it and lobs back a chip of pale green glass. He kisses it and pockets it.]
CORRA: That's a penny. That's half a loaf.
CORRA: Morning, Keep!
KEEPER: Keep?
CORRA: Keeper's too long. You're up all night. Nobody's got time for the whole word.
[She rattles the glass in her apron pocket before she goes on.]
CORRA: You want a piece? For your lamp. Hang it in the window. Catches the light lovely.
KEEPER: What is it?
CORRA: Glass. Off my pan. It was sand, and then it wasn't.
CORRA: I pick it at sun-up, before the boots get on it. Nine mornings now.
CORRA: That's nine mornings of bread, Keep. First bread we've not owed for since the bad year.
KEEPER: Who buys it?
CORRA: Boats, for luck. Carters off the packet, for a keepsake. Hester took two for her window.
CORRA: Old Pell won't. Says he's had enough off the sky this month without carrying it about.
CORRA: Pell's daft. Luck's luck. You don't ask where it fell from.
KEEPER: Is it warm?
[Her hand closes over the pocket.]
CORRA: It's not warm. It's just my pocket.
[A beat. She looks out at the boats, not at the Keeper.]
CORRA: Mum says it's the first week she's slept since the bad year. On account of the rent.
CORRA: So it's good glass. Whatever it is. It's good.
CORRA: Anyway. Mags sent me.
CORRA: Well, she sent me to the bakery. But she said if I saw a council coat, bring it.
KEEPER: Why?
CORRA: Abel's at my pan with a shovel. He wants it all back in the hole.
CORRA: Mags says somebody from the council ought to hear it. She's asked the magistrate twice.
CORRA: Twice he sent a note. "The magistrate notes your concern." Mags used it to light the stove.
KEEPER: I keep the light. I'm not the council.
CORRA: You've got the coat. Coat's what counts, out on the pans.
CORRA: And your light's lit, and the sun's up. You're off till dusk, aren't you?
[Choice a: "I was going to sleep."]
[Choice b: "Show me the pans."]
[Choices converge.]
CORRA: Sleep's free. You can do that any time. Pans are this way.
CORRA: You're new, aren't you? Up the coast, Hester says. Did they have glass up the coast?
KEEPER: No.
CORRA: Course not. It only fell here. That makes it worth more. That's how worth works.
[She stops halfway down the steps and points at the headland. A small lamp is coming down the old lighthouse stair, window by window.]
CORRA: That's Sera Vant! She's coming down!
CORRA: She never comes down before noon. She sleeps sitting up, Bram says, with the chalk in her hand.
CORRA: She's going to the crater. She said she would, at first light. I heard her tell Mags.
CORRA: Sera Vant. At my pan. Come on, Keep, or she'll get there first!
[Objective: Walk north to the salt pans.]
[Skip summary: At dawn on the ninth day after the fall, Corra Vey sells crater glass to the boats as luck, a penny a piece. Mags has sent her to fetch any council coat to the salt pans, because Abel wants the glass buried. Sera is heading to the crater too.]

### Scene 2 — The north road
[The north road out of the harbor. Cottages give way to reed beds. Ahead, a narrow upright woman in a long coat walks with a sextant at her hip and a coil of knotted line over her shoulder.]
CORRA: Sera! Sera Vant! It's me! Corra! From the rim!
SERA: I know who you are. You're loud at the rim, too.
SERA: Keeper. Good. We're going to the crater. I want it fixed by daylight, from three sides, before anyone moves another piece.
KEEPER: Fixed?
SERA: Its edge. Its width. Where it sits from the headland. A number for each.
SERA: You can hold the other end of the line. Every knot's a pace.
KEEPER: Why three sides?
SERA: One side is an opinion. Two is an argument. Three, and it can't be anywhere else.
SERA: The headland's one. The breakwater's two. The pan-house is three.
SERA: I've had one side for nine nights. In the dark, with a lamp, and Bram holding the lamp wrong.
[Corra falls into step on Sera's other side, matching her stride exactly.]
CORRA: I saw you sight it, that night! Same as you do! Like this!
[Corra holds up a fist to her eye and squints at the sky.]
SERA: Then write down where. Not that you saw me.
CORRA: Where what?
SERA: Where you were pointing. Nobody needs to know you were pointing.
CORRA: Oh. Right. Where.
[She looks at her empty fist, then up again, and says nothing for six steps.]
CORRA: What's the brass worth? On your sextant?
SERA: Nothing.
CORRA: Brass is never nothing. That's a week's rent, that is.
SERA: It's my mother's. Nothing.
CORRA: ...Fine. Fine!
CORRA: Is it true you've got a whole wall? Up the light? All chalk?
SERA: Nearly a whole wall. There's a hand's width left.
CORRA: What happens when it's full?
SERA: I find more stone.
CORRA: And when there's no more stone?
[Sera doesn't answer that. She walks a little faster.]
[Sera stops and sights the headland, then the marsh, then walks on.]
SERA: How much glass have you picked?
CORRA: Loads. There's loads.
SERA: There were loads. How many are left?
CORRA: I don't count it. I sell it. That's the point of picking it.
SERA: Then how do you know what to charge?
CORRA: I look at them. If they look rich, it's tuppence.
CORRA: Fewer. There's fewer.
SERA: Fewer is a number. Find it.
CORRA: How do I find fewer?
SERA: Count what's there tomorrow. Take it from what's there today. That's fewer, with a number on.
SERA: Every piece you pick is a piece I can't count.
CORRA: It's off my pan, though. It's not off your sky.
SERA: It came off my sky, and then it landed on your pan. That's two places. I want both.
KEEPER: What does Abel want?
CORRA: Abel wants it buried. All of it. And the hole filled in, and the reeds cut, and nobody to talk about it ever.
CORRA: He says you'll count it, and they'll call it haze, and he'll be stuck living next to it.
SERA: He's right about the middle part.
CORRA: You're on my side, though. Aren't you? You don't want it buried.
SERA: I don't want it buried. I don't want it sold. I want it left where it fell until I've had it.
CORRA: That's not a side. That's a third side.
SERA: Then it's mine.
KEEPER: How's Wren?
SERA: Asleep, when I left. Bram's on the step with her.
SERA: She asked me at dusk what morning was for. I said light. She said, "Is that all it does?"
SERA: I didn't have a better answer. I've been thinking about it since.
[Sera walks on ahead. Corra slows down beside the Keeper and lowers her voice.]
CORRA: She talks to you different. Like you're a number she likes.
KEEPER: Is that good?
CORRA: It's better than me. I'm a number she's still checking.
CORRA: She'll get to me. She checks everything in the end. That's what Bram says.
CORRA: He says she checked his stew for a month before she ate it. Now she just holds it.
[Objective: Reach the pan-house.]
[Skip summary: On the north road the keeper and Corra catch up with Sera, who wants the crater fixed by daylight before anyone moves more glass. Corra wants it sold, Abel wants it buried, and Sera wants it counted: a third side.]

### Scene 3 — The pan-house
[A long low shed of grey boards at the edge of the salt pans. White crusted pans run off toward the reeds. On the door, a chalked tide table, smudged and old. A broad, tired woman sits on the step with a dented tin box on her knees and a pencil behind her ear.]
[She takes out the pencil and taps it twice on the tin before she speaks.]
MAGS: Corra Vey. That's not bread.
CORRA: Bakery was shut.
MAGS: Bakery's never shut. It's a bakery.
MAGS: Corra, me, the Dunn boys on the far pans, old Ferris asleep in the shed. Abel's at your pan, by the sound of it.
MAGS: That's my lot. And a surveyor. And a coat.
MAGS: Good. The coat'll do.
KEEPER: You asked for the council.
MAGS: I asked for the magistrate. Twice. Got a note twice.
MAGS: So I asked Corra for anybody in a council coat, and here you are, and you look like you've not slept.
KEEPER: I haven't.
MAGS: Nobody on the pans has, since. Sit. Not there. That's Abel's step, and he's particular.
MAGS: That night I was in here counting the day's salt. Flash came through the door like somebody opened the sun.
MAGS: I took the lamp and went out and counted heads. Corra was the only one short. Found her coming back up the bank.
MAGS: Knocked flat, she said. Couldn't stop talking. I sent her home with a blanket and a docked share.
CORRA: You never docked it.
MAGS: I said I docked it. Saying's half the docking.
[Sera has already gone to the door and is rubbing at the old tide table with her sleeve.]
MAGS: Leave my door alone, Surveyor.
SERA: It's two years old. I told Corra to tell you to take it down.
MAGS: She told me. It's the only thing on that door that isn't a bill.
SERA: It's wrong.
MAGS: It's paid for. That's nearly the same, out here.
[Sera chalks a small correction in the corner of the table anyway. Mags lets her.]
MAGS: Right. Here's the whole of it, Keeper, so you can say you heard it.
MAGS: Nine days of glass. Corra picks it off her pan and sells it at the slip. The pan-house takes a share.
MAGS: Pan-house share, Corra. Fair's fair.
CORRA: Fair's whatever the one holding the tin says.
MAGS: And I'm holding it. So that's fair.
MAGS: Corra's mum has the rent this month. First time since the bad year.
MAGS: The Dunn boys have boots. Old Ferris has coal ordered for the shed, and he'll be warm in it.
MAGS: That's glass money. All of it.
KEEPER: And Abel?
MAGS: Abel Thorn's had fifty years in the reeds and thirty on these pans with me.
MAGS: He's never once been wrong about weather. Not once. I've lost bets on it.
MAGS: And he wants it buried. Every piece, and the hole filled, and him with the shovel.
KEEPER: What do you want?
[Mags taps the pencil on the tin. She doesn't answer that.]
MAGS: I want somebody in a council coat to hear both of them.
MAGS: So when it goes wrong, I can say I asked.
KEEPER: And the magistrate's notes?
MAGS: "The magistrate notes your concern." Lovely hand. Good paper. Burns slow.
MAGS: Ivo Thane's never once said no to my face. He sends paper. Paper's how he says no.
KEEPER: Will it go wrong?
MAGS: Something always does. Knowing who to blame is half of running a pan.
[A scrape of iron on glass carries from across the pans, then a girl's shout from nowhere near it.]
CORRA: That's my pan! He's on my pan!
[Corra is already running.]
MAGS: There it goes. Help me up, Keeper. My feet have opinions about running.
[Objective: Go to Corra's pan.]
[Skip summary: At the pan-house, Mags Teller explains: the glass money has paid rent, boots and coal, and Abel wants every piece buried. Ivo has refused her twice, so she wants the keeper, a council coat, to hear both sides. Abel starts shovelling Corra's pan.]

### Scene 4 — Corra's pan
[A salt pan near the reeds, its crust broken and glittering with pale glass. An old man in a reed-cutter's smock is shovelling glass into a cart. A shaggy pony stands in the traces, eating nothing in particular.]
[Corra plants herself in front of the cart.]
CORRA: That's my pan! That's my glass! Abel!
[Abel stops. He runs his thumb along the edge of the reed-hook at his belt before he speaks.]
ABEL: Put it back, child. Marsh gives, marsh keeps.
CORRA: Marsh never gave me a thing. This did.
ABEL: Marsh gives, marsh keeps. Meaning, put it back.
CORRA: I know what it means! You've said it since I was six!
ABEL: Since you were six, and you listened then.
ABEL: I put a hook in your hand when you were six. I'll not see you cut by this.
CORRA: It's glass, Abel. It's not a dog. It doesn't bite.
ABEL: It was sand an hour before. I've seen weather. Not this. But weather.
CORRA: You'd keep me poor to keep the marsh the way you like it.
ABEL: I'd keep you. That's all. Poor or not.
CORRA: Mum's rent's paid. First time in a year. Do you know what that's like? Her sleeping?
ABEL: I know what it's like when she doesn't. I've sat with her. You were too little to remember.
[Corra opens her mouth, and shuts it, and rattles the pocket instead.]
CORRA: That's not fair.
ABEL: No. Marsh isn't, child. That's why I'm careful with it.
[Mags arrives, breathing hard. Sera comes behind her, already walking the edge of the pan with the line.]
MAGS: Abel. You said you'd wait till I'd a coat.
ABEL: You've a coat. I've a cart.
ABEL: Patch has pulled it twice round the pans already. There's my girl.
[He pats the pony's neck. The pony leans into his hand.]
SERA: Don't tip it. Any of it.
ABEL: Surveyor.
SERA: Every piece you move is a piece off my count. Leave it where it fell.
ABEL: You'll count it.
SERA: Yes.
ABEL: Nine days you've been counting the sky. Is it still where it ought to be?
SERA: No. It's four degrees east.
ABEL: Then counting never filled a hole.
SERA: Burying never told anyone where one was.
ABEL: Maybe nobody needs telling.
SERA: Then why are you on Corra's pan at sun-up with a cart?
ABEL: Because somebody's got to do it, and the council won't.
SERA: The council won't count it either. That makes two of us they won't help.
[A beat. Neither of them moves.]
MAGS: Corra. Abel. Surveyor. Three of you, and one pan.
MAGS: Here's fair, and you'll none of you like it.

MAGS: Well, Keeper? You heard both. Three, with the Surveyor.

### Scene 5 — The north channel
[A tidal channel between the pans and the crater, brown water to the knee. In the middle of it stands something tall and thin, perfectly still, facing the crater. The reeds around it all lean the same way.]
[Patch stops dead in the traces. Abel stops with her.]
ABEL: Stood there since sun-up. Doesn't move if you don't.
KEEPER: You leave it there?
ABEL: Stand still for what stands still. Meaning, go round.
ABEL: Round's the long way, and I've an old pony. But round.
CORRA: Best glass on the whole pan is just past it. On the far bank. I saw it at sun-up.
MAGS: That's past the reeds, Corra. That's the bowl side. We've just said.
CORRA: It's the bank. It's not the bowl. It's a foot of water.
SERA: Corra. Don't.
SERA: It hasn't moved in how long, Abel?
ABEL: Since sun-up. Six hours.
SERA: Then don't be the thing that changes.
KEEPER: What happens if we wait?
ABEL: Tide turns at four. Channel fills. It stands in the deep instead. Then nobody crosses till morning.
MAGS: And we lose the afternoon, and the afternoon's my wages. Everybody's wages.
SERA: Then we lose the afternoon. It's six hours of standing still. I'd like to know what seven looks like.
KEEPER: Has anyone touched it?
ABEL: Dunn boy threw a clod at one Tuesday. Out by the far pans.
ABEL: It turned round. That's all I'll say about Tuesday. He's not thrown a clod since.
MAGS: He's not been past the third pan since, either. I've had to move his whole share.
CORRA: It's only a foot of water!
CORRA: I'll go quiet. I'll go round its back. It hasn't got a front, anyway. How would it know?
[Corra steps into the channel, between the tall figure and the crater. It turns its faceless head toward her. Then all of it turns.]
[Two more step down out of the haze on the far bank.]
MAGS: Corra! Behind me! Now!
ABEL: Steady, my girl. Steady.

KEEPER: Are you all right?

### Scene 6 — The crater rim, noon
[The bowl of fused glass, pale and quiet at noon. The reeds round it lean in toward the middle. Sera stands on the rim with the knotted line. The Keeper holds the other end.]
[Objective: Hold the line for Sera.]
SERA: Pull it tight. Tighter. Hold.
SERA: Thirty-two paces. Rim to rim, north to south.
SERA: Again. East to west.
[They cross the rim the long way round. Sera counts knots under her breath.]
SERA: Thirty-two. Round, then. Near enough exact.
SERA: Nine days ago I had one count of this place. My own. At night, with a lamp.
SERA: Now I have its width, in daylight, with a second pair of hands. That's a fixed thing.
KEEPER: What's it for?
SERA: For the wall. For the next time somebody calls it haze.
KEEPER: Will they listen this time?
SERA: No. They didn't listen to four degrees. They won't listen to thirty-two paces.
SERA: But it'll be on the wall. And the wall doesn't need them to listen.
[She winds the line in, knot by knot, without looking at it.]
SERA: My mother said a number doesn't care who believes it. I used to think that was a comfort.
KEEPER: Isn't it?
SERA: It's true. That's different.
SERA: I told the council four degrees. I'll tell them thirty-two paces. Next week it'll be something else.
SERA: One day I'll run out of things to tell them, and they'll still be calling it haze.
[Abel stands at the reed line with Patch, his back half turned to the bowl. He doesn't step onto the glass.]
SERA: Abel. Come and look.
ABEL: I'll not stand on it.
SERA: Then count from there. How many reeds are leaning?
ABEL: All of them, Surveyor.
SERA: All is not a number.
ABEL: It is out here.
SERA: When did they start?
ABEL: Before. You know that. I said it that night.
SERA: Before by how long?
ABEL: Two prayers. I've told you. I'll not say more on it.
[He runs his thumb along the reed-hook. He doesn't say anything else.]
SERA: Nobody out here will give me a number.
KEEPER: I gave you thirty-two.
SERA: You held the line. That's half of thirty-two.
SERA: It's the better half. I can't hold both ends.
[Corra has come up behind them. She stays at the edge of the glass, well back, and doesn't look down into it.]
[The Keeper goes to the reed line where Abel stands. He doesn't look round.]
ABEL: You've a council coat and an honest face. That's a bad mix out here.
KEEPER: Why?
ABEL: Folk will tell you things. And then you'll have to carry them.
ABEL: I carried the reeds, that night. I've carried them nine days. They don't get lighter.
KEEPER: What did you see?
ABEL: I've said. They leaned. Then it came down where they were pointing.
ABEL: That's all I saw. That's all I'll say. Ask me again, I'll say it the same.
[He turns Patch's head away from the bowl, gently, with one hand.]
ABEL: There's my girl. Don't look. Nothing in there for you.
CORRA: Sera. Can I ask something? Not about glass.
SERA: Ask.
CORRA: The girl. In there. That night. Is she all right?
SERA: She's called Wren. Bram named her. She eats when he tells her to, which is more than I do.
CORRA: Wren. Like the bird.
CORRA: What's she like?
SERA: She asks questions. All of them. Whether supper's a kind of star.
CORRA: Does she... remember? Being out here? Anyone out here?
SERA: She remembers nothing before the glass.
[Corra breathes out.]
CORRA: Good. That's... good. For her, I mean.
SERA: Why?
CORRA: No reason. It's cold out here, is all. Nobody wants to remember cold.
[Sera looks at her a moment longer than she needs to. Then she turns back to the bowl.]
CORRA: You said, "Learn, then come." Up your light. I've been counting.
SERA: To what?
CORRA: Four hundred. Twice. In my head, on the pan.
SERA: In your head doesn't count. Out loud, with someone listening.
CORRA: Who?
SERA: Anyone who'll say you got there. Learn. Then come. That hasn't changed.
[Sera kneels on the rim and chalks the number on a flat piece of glass: 32.]
SERA: Keeper. Count the leaning reeds on the east side, from here to the channel.
[Choice a: "I'll count them."]
[Choice b: "Abel's right. It's all of them."]
[Choices converge.]
[The Keeper looks along the east side. Every reed, as far as the channel, leans toward the bowl.]
SERA: All of them.
SERA: Then I'll write "all."
[She chalks it on the glass beside the number, in small square letters.]
SERA: I hate it.
ABEL: That's the first true thing you've said about this place, Surveyor.
[Objective: Go back to the pan-house.]
[Skip summary: On the crater rim at noon, Sera paces the crater with the keeper: thirty-two paces across. Abel won't stand on the glass or give her a count, only "all." Corra asks after the girl from the crater and is relieved to hear she remembers nothing.]

### Scene 7 — The pan-house, dinner
[Inside the pan-house. Salt sacks along one wall, a stove, a long table scarred with tally marks. Mags cuts a loaf into equal shares with a bread knife and lines them up like coins.]
[Objective: Eat, and take the pan-house post.]
MAGS: Sit. Eat. Corra, that's yours. Surveyor, that's yours.
SERA: Bram feeds me at dusk.
MAGS: Then he can feed you at dusk as well. Eat that.
[Sera takes the share. She holds it. She doesn't eat it.]
MAGS: Keeper, that's yours. You've had a morning.
CORRA: That's a farthing of bread, that is. And the fish is two farthings. Mags, that's three farthings each.
MAGS: It's dinner. It's not for sale.
CORRA: Everything's for sale. It's only some things nobody's bought yet.
KEEPER: Where are the others?
MAGS: Dunn boys ate theirs at eleven, standing up. They'll be hungry again by two.
MAGS: Old Ferris won't wake till the salt's dry. I leave his on the sack. It goes.
[Mags takes the heel of the loaf for herself. She breaks it in half and gives half to the pony through the open door.]
CORRA: You never take a proper share, Mags.
MAGS: I take the heel. Somebody has to take the heel. Otherwise it's waste.
MAGS: Corra, you're going to the slip this afternoon?
CORRA: Packet's in at four. Packet people buy anything. Last packet, a woman bought three pieces for her hens.
KEEPER: Her hens?
CORRA: For laying. She said it'd make them lay. I said it would. They're her hens.
MAGS: That's how you sell, is it.
CORRA: That's how everybody sells. I'm just quicker.
SERA: Did they lay?
CORRA: What?
SERA: The hens. Did they lay more, after?
CORRA: How would I know? She went back on the packet.
SERA: Then you sold her a number you didn't have.
CORRA: I sold her a piece of glass. The number was her idea.
[Sera considers that. She takes one bite of the bread, which surprises everyone, including Sera.]
MAGS: Well. Mark that on a wall somewhere.
KEEPER: Where's your family, Mags?
MAGS: This lot. And a sister in the hills who writes once a year to tell me I'm too fat to run a pan.
MAGS: She's right. My feet agree with her. I don't let them vote.
CORRA: Mags gets letters from her sister and reads them out at dinner. In a voice.
MAGS: A very particular voice. My sister has a very particular nose. One day I'll do it for you, Keeper.
SERA: Is it accurate?
MAGS: To the inch, Surveyor.
[Mags pulls three letters from under the pay-tin and pushes them across to the Keeper.]
MAGS: You're the mail, aren't you? Keeper carries the post to the packet. Always has.
MAGS: This one's to my sister in the hills. This one's the salt order.
MAGS: This one's a bill, and it can sink.
KEEPER: You want me to sink it?
MAGS: I want you to put it in the bag like an honest coat. I want the packet to sink it. That's different.
CORRA: Who's it from?
MAGS: Coal man. For last winter's coal. Which we burnt. Which is the trouble with coal.
KEEPER: Where's Abel?
MAGS: Took the reed cart to town. He's got a load of thatch to sell, and a speech for the magistrate.
MAGS: He'll make it on the slip, where everybody can hear. He only makes one a year.
SERA: I'm going to the slip as well. The table there wants a mark.
MAGS: Then go and hear Abel, Keeper. Somebody should hear Abel when he makes a speech.
MAGS: And Surveyor. Eat that before you go.
SERA: I'm holding it.
MAGS: Holding's not eating. Bram Hollis says so. The whole harbor's heard him.
[Sera puts the bread in her coat pocket. Mags sighs and lets her.]
[Objective: Take the post to the slip.]
[Skip summary: At dinner in the pan-house, Mags shares out the bread and takes only the heel. She gives the keeper the pan-house letters for the packet. Abel has gone to town to make a speech to the magistrate on the slip, and Sera and Corra are heading there too.]

### Scene 8 — The slip
[The harbor slip below the customs house. Sera's tide table is nailed to a post. A small crowd waits for the packet: a carter, a woman with a crate of hens, two boys on a bollard. Abel's reed cart stands at the top of the slip, Patch dozing in the traces. Corra works the crowd with her apron.]
[On the customs house steps, a tall gloved man in a magistrate's coat holds a sheaf of papers. He doesn't look up as the Keeper comes.]
[Objective: Hand the mail to the magistrate.]
IVO: Keeper. You have the mail.
IVO: You also have salt on your boots, which is not the way from the breakwater.
KEEPER: Mags asked for a council coat.
IVO: She asked twice. I declined twice. Persistence is a virtue in salt. It is less of one in a harbor.
[He takes the bag, checks the seal, and hands it on to the packet clerk without opening it.]
IVO: Sleep is also a virtue, Keeper. You light the lamp in four hours.
KEEPER: Abel's coming to see you.
IVO: Mr Thorn has been coming to see me since before you were born. He comes once a year. It is usually about drains.
KEEPER: It isn't about drains.
IVO: No. I did not expect it would be.
[At the tide-table post, Sera chalks a mark. She doesn't turn round.]
SERA: Keeper.
KEEPER: Yes?
SERA: Tell the magistrate the crater is thirty-two paces across. I paced it at noon.
IVO: The magistrate is standing on the step, Surveyor. He heard.
SERA: Keeper. Tell him he can write it down.
[Ivo writes nothing down. He looks at the papers in his hand.]
[Abel walks down from his cart, slow, thumb on his reed-hook. The crowd turns to watch him come.]
ABEL: Magistrate.
IVO: Mr Thorn. You are a long way from your reeds.
ABEL: There's a hole in the marsh. Glass in the bottom. Council should fill it.
IVO: The council has received a note from the pan-house on the subject. Two notes.
ABEL: I'm not a note. I'm here.

ABEL: Fifty years I've cut that marsh. My father before me, and his.
ABEL: Reeds lean to water. To the sun, a little. Never to a hole in the ground.
ABEL: They lean to it now. Every one. No wind.
ABEL: There's figures stood in the channels. Tall. They don't move till you cross them. A Dunn boy saw. I've seen.
ABEL: Marsh gives, marsh keeps. Meaning, you don't take from it what you can't put back.
ABEL: Fill it. Bury the glass. I'll do the shovelling. Give me two men and a week.
[Somebody in the crowd laughs, uneasily, and stops.]
[Corra, in the crowd, is holding up a chip of glass to a carter.]
CORRA: Luck glass! Fell out of the sky! Penny a piece!
CARTER: Is it right, Magistrate? Something come down on the pans? Out the sky?
[The crowd goes quiet. The woman with the hens holds her crate a little tighter.]
[Ivo raises his voice, only enough for it to carry.]
IVO: Nothing fell on Saltreach.
IVO: We had haze, and a hot night on the pans, and the pans threw up glass. That is all it is.
IVO: Buy it if it pleases you. It will not make your boat luckier, and it will not sink it.
[A few people laugh. The woman with the hens loosens her grip. The boys on the bollard lose interest.]
CARTER: Haze, then. Told my missus it was haze.
IVO: Then your missus has been well informed, and you may tell her the magistrate said so.
[Corra holds her chip of glass up between two fingers and looks at it as if the price had changed.]
ABEL: I saw it come down.
IVO: You saw a light in the haze, Mr Thorn. So did half the harbor. Light in haze is a strange thing.
ABEL: And the figures in the channels?
IVO: Fog stands up in a marsh, Mr Thorn. You of all men know that. It stands very tall at dusk.
IVO: I would not cross a channel in it either. That is sense, not sorcery.
IVO: The council will not pay men to bury glass. It would be paying men to bury sand.
ABEL: Then I'll bury it myself.
IVO: That is your right, on your own pan. On Mrs Teller's, you will need Mrs Teller.
[Abel looks at him a long time. Then he turns and walks back up to his cart.]
[Sera chalks one more mark on the post. Her hand is very steady.]
SERA: Keeper. Thirty-two paces. Write that in your book.
SERA: Somebody's book should have it.
[She walks off along the quay toward the headland without looking back.]

KEEPER: Why won't she speak to you?
IVO: Because I called her numbers haze in open council, with her standing in front of it.
IVO: She has a long memory, Keeper. And a longer wall.

IVO: You were at the crater. Was there anything there but glass?

[Choice b: "Ask the surveyor."]
[Choices converge.]
IVO: I asked you.
IVO: Never mind. It was not a fair question to put to a coat with salt on its boots.

[Objective: Meet the packet.]

### Scene 9 — The slip, the packet in
[The packet ties up alongside. Sacks, a crate of lamp oil, a goat. The first passenger down the plank is a slight man in a good southern coat, with a small case and a watch-chain. On the chain hangs a little brass bell.]
[He touches the bell without ringing it. He looks round the harbor, at the smokehouse, at the tower on the headland, up once at the sky, and back down.]
TEODOR: Ah. So this is Saltreach. Smaller than one is told, and a great deal more fish.
[He nods to the Keeper as the mailbag goes up the plank past him.]
TEODOR: Good afternoon. You'll be the harbor post, I think. A fine bag. It's a good sign, a full bag. It means people still write.
[He sees Corra's apron and crosses to her, slowly, as if not to frighten anything.]
TEODOR: Forgive me. I'm told there may be glass to be had here. Off the salt pans.
CORRA: Luck glass. Penny a piece. Fell out of the... it's off my pan.
TEODOR: May I?
[Corra holds out a piece. He takes it in two fingers and turns it to the light for a long time.]
TEODOR: A lovely piece. Truly. One sees the sand still in it, if one looks.
TEODOR: I'll pay what it's worth.
CORRA: Penny.
TEODOR: Then I'm afraid we disagree, you and I. I should say a shilling.
[Corra stares at him. Somebody in the crowd whistles.]
CORRA: A shilling's a pair of boots.
TEODOR: Then I hope they're good boots, Miss...?
CORRA: Vey. Corra Vey.
TEODOR: Miss Vey.
[He puts a shilling in her palm and closes her fingers over it, very lightly.]
CORRA: Nobody calls me Miss.
TEODOR: Then Saltreach has been careless. How many pieces have you, Miss Vey?
CORRA: A pocket. And a pan.
TEODOR: I should very much like to see the pan, if you'll allow me. And whoever sets the price.
CORRA: That's Mags. Mrs Teller. She's got the tin.
TEODOR: Then I shall need to be very polite to Mrs Teller.
CORRA: Won't help. She's not polite back. She's fair. It's worse.
TEODOR: Ah. I've met fair people. You're quite right. It's very much worse.
[Ivo has come down the customs house steps. He stops a pace from the stranger.]
IVO: You are buying, sir.
TEODOR: Magistrate. I'd guess magistrate, from the coat. Teodor Lisle.
TEODOR: From Bellwater, south of you, some days down the coast. I'm a buyer of glass, and of very little else.
IVO: We do not often see a Bellwater coat on the slip.
TEODOR: No. It's a long way to come for fish. Glass is another matter.
IVO: You heard of our glass in Bellwater.
TEODOR: One hears things on a quay. Glass on the salt pans at Saltreach. I came as soon as I heard.
IVO: As soon as.
IVO: The packet calls twice a week. Word of our pans could have reached Bellwater in three days.
IVO: A man in a hurry would be here in six. It is nine.
[Teodor touches the bell on his watch-chain. He smiles, very slightly.]
TEODOR: Then I'm not a man in a hurry, Magistrate.
TEODOR: News travels at its own pace. As you know.
IVO: Noted.
IVO: Where will you lodge, Mr Lisle?
TEODOR: I'm told there's a room over a smokehouse. I'm told it's the only room.
IVO: It is. Mrs Pike will feed you and remember everything you say.
TEODOR: Then I shall say very little, and eat a great deal. It's how I like to travel.
[Ivo lets a moment go by.]
IVO: You will hear in the smokehouse that something fell from the sky. It did not. We had haze.
TEODOR: Of course.
TEODOR: There has been a great deal of haze about this autumn. We have had our share.
IVO: In Bellwater.
TEODOR: Where I'm from, Magistrate, there is always weather. It is very nearly all we talk about.
[Corra holds up a second piece before anyone can stop her.]
CORRA: This one's better. Bigger. Pay what the sailors pay. And a bit.
TEODOR: The sailors pay a penny, I believe. So, a penny and a bit.
CORRA: No! I meant... a shilling and a bit!
TEODOR: Ah. Then a shilling and a bit, Miss Vey. I'd not want you to say I cheated you.
[Abel has come down from his cart and stands at Ivo's shoulder, watching Teodor count coins into Corra's hand for the second piece.]
ABEL: You don't like him either.
IVO: I have not decided. That is worse for him.
IVO: Mr Thorn, you are going home. Mr Lisle wants the pan-house. Mrs Teller will want to see him in daylight.
IVO: Keeper. You have until dusk. Walk with them.
KEEPER: Why me?
IVO: Because you are a council coat with nothing to sell. They are rare on the north road.
TEODOR: How kind. I shall try not to be any trouble.
ABEL: Then don't come.
TEODOR: Ah. I'm afraid I've paid for the room.

### Scene 10 — The ford
[The north road, mid-afternoon. Abel walks at Patch's head. Teodor walks on the other side of the cart, his case in one hand. The Keeper walks behind.]
TEODOR: Your marsh is very beautiful, Mr Thorn. I've not seen reeds that color since I was a boy.
ABEL: They're brown.
TEODOR: A great many browns. One forgets how many.
ABEL: You'll not flatter my reeds into a price.
TEODOR: No, indeed. I'd never insult a reed by buying it.
[Patch swings her head round and pushes her nose into Teodor's coat pocket. Teodor stands quite still and lets her.]
TEODOR: Ah. Hello. I'm afraid I've nothing in there but a pencil.
ABEL: Patch. Leave it.
[Patch does not leave it. She breathes on Teodor's hand.]
ABEL: My pony likes you. I don't hold with it.
TEODOR: Animals are generous, Mr Thorn. I try to deserve it.
ABEL: She liked a tinker once. He took the kettle.
TEODOR: Then I shall stay well away from your kettle.
KEEPER: Is Bellwater far?
TEODOR: Far enough, Keeper. A long way south, down a long coast.
TEODOR: There's a great deal of rain there this time of year. Very warm rain. One gets used to it.
KEEPER: What's it like?
TEODOR: Wet, mostly. We have a bell tower. It's very old, and very fond of itself.
